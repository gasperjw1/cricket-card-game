# Academic ball-by-ball cricket simulation models (per-delivery outcome distributions)

Scope: statistical models that output a probability distribution over the outcome of each delivery (0/1/2/3/4/6/wicket/extras) as a function of batter, bowler and match situation, plus the resource/value-function models (D/L/S, WASP) that can be used to calibrate such a simulator, and the Test-cricket survival/hazard literature. Written so a developer can re-implement each model without the paper. Primary sources were read in full where author PDFs exist (all SFU papers, both Stevenson/Brewer papers, Brewer 2008, Perera/Gill/Swartz declaration paper, Brooker's PhD thesis). Where only abstracts were reachable this is stated.

A reading-order recommendation for an engine designer is at the very end of the Inferences for Question 2.

## Q1. Swartz, Gill, Beaudoin & de Silva (2006) "Optimal batting orders in one-day cricket" and Swartz, Gill & Muthukumarana (2009) "Modelling and simulation for one-day cricket"

### Takeaway
Swartz et al. (2006) is the foundational per-ball model: a 7-category multinomial (0,1,2,3,4,6,wicket) whose log-odds are a batter-specific baseline plus common linear effects of wickets lost, balls bowled and the Duckworth-Lewis resource percentage, fitted in WinBUGS; extras are generated by two fixed Bernoulli rates outside the multinomial. Full parameter table for 13 Indian batsmen is published. The 2009 Canadian Journal of Statistics follow-up (Bayesian latent variable model adding bowler and innings) is paywalled; I could only recover its abstract and later authors' descriptions of it.

### Cited Findings

**Swartz, Gill, Beaudoin, de Silva (2006), Computers & Operations Research 33:1939-1950, doi 10.1016/j.cor.2004.09.031** — author PDF: [optorders.pdf](https://www.sfu.ca/~tswartz/papers/optorders.pdf)

- Outcome space: X_i for ball i in {0,1,2,3,4,5,6} coded as 0,1,2,3,4 runs; code 5 = six runs; code 6 = dismissal. Five-run outcomes are ignored. Wides/no-balls/byes/leg-byes are handled outside the multinomial. — [optorders.pdf](https://www.sfu.ca/~tswartz/papers/optorders.pdf)
- Simulation algorithm (first innings, 300 legal balls): for each ball draw u1~U(0,1); if u1 < q1 (wide/no-ball) add 1 run and do NOT count the ball (repeat); else if u1 < q1+q2 (bye/leg-bye) add 1 run and count the ball; else draw X_i from the batter's multinomial for the current (wickets, balls) state and add X_i·I(X_i<=4) + 6·I(X_i=5). Estimated from 239 ODI first innings up to May 2003: q1 = 0.0356 (wides+no-balls per ball), q2 = 0.0208 (byes+leg-byes per ball). The authors note the treatment "could be improved slightly by allowing the possibility of more than one run" per extra. — [optorders.pdf](https://www.sfu.ca/~tswartz/papers/optorders.pdf)
- Covariates considered: batter j, wickets lost w (0..9), balls bowled b (0..299). Bowler, opposition, coach, pitch are listed as relevant but excluded ("we consider the first three factors"). — [optorders.pdf](https://www.sfu.ca/~tswartz/papers/optorders.pdf)
- Model: T_jwb0..T_jwb6 ~ Multinomial(N_jwb; p_jwb0..p_jwb6) with p_jwbk = q_jwbk / Σ_k q_jwbk and the log-linear form
  log(q_jwbk) = μ_jk + α_k·(w/9) + β_k·(b/299) + γ_k·(R(w,b)/100)
  where R(w,b) is the Duckworth-Lewis percentage of resources *used* (R(0,0)=0, R(10,b)=100), acting as a wickets×balls interaction term. Identifiability constraints: α_6 = β_6 = γ_6 = 0 and μ_j6 = 0 for every batter (dismissal is the reference category). Covariates are standardised to [0,1] so μ_jk is the batter's baseline at the start of the innings. Key assumption: "batsmen have different abilities, yet as the match progresses we assume that their characteristics change in a common manner." — [optorders.pdf](https://www.sfu.ca/~tswartz/papers/optorders.pdf)
- Priors: μ_jk ~ N(0, σ_k²), α_k ~ N(0, σ_α²), β_k ~ N(0, σ_β²), γ_k ~ N(0, σ_γ²), each precision ~ Gamma(0.001, 0.001) (diffuse). Fitted in WinBUGS; posterior means used as point estimates. — [optorders.pdf](https://www.sfu.ca/~tswartz/papers/optorders.pdf)
- Data: 71 first-innings ODIs for India, Dec 1998 – Mar 2003; 13 batsmen; balls collapsed into 5-over bins ("batting characteristics do not change substantively within these narrow categories"), giving 404 multinomial cells; 13·6+6+6+6 = 96 primary parameters. — [optorders.pdf](https://www.sfu.ca/~tswartz/papers/optorders.pdf)
- Published parameter estimates (Table 1; k = 0,1,2,3,4,5(=six); k=6 fixed at 0):
  - Common: α_k = (1.49, 1.41, −0.34, −0.28, −1.19, −2.04); β_k = (−0.35, 4.22, 0.25, −1.33, −1.04, 1.91); γ_k = (−2.44, −4.54, 0.29, −1.02, 1.13, 0.50).
  - Batter baselines μ_jk: Tendulkar (4.15, 2.56, 1.40, −0.26, 2.12, −1.41); Sehwag (3.30, 1.29, 0.50, −0.63, 1.67, −1.61); Ganguly (4.06, 2.37, 0.86, −0.26, 1.70, −0.79); Kaif (3.59, 1.86, 0.33, −0.23, 1.24, −2.43); Dravid (4.30, 2.56, 1.10, −0.04, 1.77, −2.29); Yuvraj Singh (3.84, 1.67, 0.50, −0.17, 1.42, −1.79); Mongia (3.68, 1.91, 0.49, −0.19, 1.54, −2.05); Harbhajan Singh (3.03, 0.81, −0.05, −0.29, 1.28, −2.08); Z. Khan (3.36, 0.44, 0.13, −0.25, 1.06, −1.13); Srinath (3.55, 1.01, −0.77, 0.04, 0.89, −2.33); Nehra (3.60, 0.93, −0.68, −0.07, −0.89, −2.22); Kumble (4.01, 1.38, −0.24, 0.21, 0.45, −3.54); Agarkar (3.31, 1.21, 0.09, −0.32, 1.06, −2.13).
  - Interpretation given by authors: as wickets are lost, α for 2s, 3s, 4s, 6s are negative (batsmen become conservative). — [optorders.pdf](https://www.sfu.ca/~tswartz/papers/optorders.pdf)
- Validation: 71,000 simulated first innings with India's 2003 World Cup final order vs. 71 actual innings; Q-Q plot of runs "fairly good" except a cluster of actual scores 122–125; wickets-lost histogram shapes agree except a spike at 6 actual wickets. No numeric calibration statistic reported. Mean simulated first-innings runs for the actual 2003 WC final order: 250.1; best annealed orders 256.4 and 256.0; reversed order 201.2. — [optorders.pdf](https://www.sfu.ca/~tswartz/papers/optorders.pdf)
- Optimisation: simulated annealing over permutations; n=10,000 simulated innings per evaluation gives SE ≈ 0.5 runs; cooling t = 0.5·(0.9)^k, k=0..14, 100 iterations per plateau; neighbourhood = randomly permute a block of m=4 adjacent positions; 7.5 h per run on a 2004 Sun workstation. — [optorders.pdf](https://www.sfu.ca/~tswartz/papers/optorders.pdf)
- Code: not published (WinBUGS used; "our software could be a valuable asset to teams"). — [optorders.pdf](https://www.sfu.ca/~tswartz/papers/optorders.pdf)

**Swartz, Gill & Muthukumarana (2009), Canadian Journal of Statistics 37(2):143-160, doi 10.1002/cjs.10017** — [publisher record](https://doi.org/10.1002/cjs.10017); closed access (Semantic Scholar: abstract elided, "CLOSED", 60 citations)

- From the paper's abstract (French version surfaced by search): outcome probabilities depend on the batsman, the bowler, wickets lost, balls bowled and the innings; the simulator is described as producing realistic results. — [search result quoting abstract](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/227761043_Modelling_and_simulation_for_one-day_cricket)
- Davis, Perera & Swartz (2015) describe it as "a Bayesian latent variable model which provided batting outcome probabilities in one-day cricket" and criticise that "they use a coarse discretization of wickets lost and overs consumed based on 9 overall categories. In particular, their structure does not account for powerplays." — [t20sim.pdf](https://www.sfu.ca/~tswartz/papers/t20sim.pdf)
- The second-innings aggressiveness mechanism in Davis et al. (2015) is explicitly "borrowed from Swartz, Gill & Muthukumarana 2009": compare the batter's current runs-per-resource ratio to the required (target − score + 1)/resources-remaining and move the batter to a later-over characteristic until the ratio is met (details under Q2). — [t20sim.pdf](https://www.sfu.ca/~tswartz/papers/t20sim.pdf)
- Perera, Davis & Swartz (2016) summarise the 2006 paper as "batting characteristics from multinomial regression" that "failed to look at the effect of bowling". — [lineups.pdf](https://www.sfu.ca/~tswartz/papers/lineups.pdf)

### Inferences
- The 2006 model is a multinomial logit with a batter random intercept per outcome and three shared slopes per outcome; it can be reproduced with any multinomial-logit library in minutes given ball-by-ball data. The D/L resource term is the only interaction, so the surface over (wickets, balls) is smooth and monotone per outcome.
- "Latent variable" for the 2009 CJS model most plausibly means an ordinal-probit style construction (a latent continuous aggressiveness with cutpoints mapping to 0..6/wicket), since Brooker's thesis and the follow-on Koulis/Muthukumarana paper use ordered probits for the same outcome, but I could not verify this from the paper itself (see Gaps).
- The 2006 published μ/α/β/γ table is directly usable to seed a game engine's "batter archetype" vectors for ODI-style cricket, with the caveat that it reflects 1998–2003 scoring rates.

### Gaps
- Full text of the 2009 CJS paper (exact latent-variable structure, how bowler effect enters, the 9 situational categories, validation numbers) was not obtainable: Wiley is paywalled, Semantic Scholar has no abstract, ResearchGate returned HTTP 403, and no author preprint exists on Swartz's or Muthukumarana's pages.
- The 2006 paper gives no numeric goodness-of-fit statistic beyond the Q-Q and wicket histograms.

## Q2. Davis, Perera & Swartz (2015) "A simulator for Twenty20 cricket" (ANZJS 57:55-71) and "Player evaluation in Twenty20 cricket" (JSA 1(1):19-31)

### Takeaway
The T20 simulator is an 8-outcome multinomial per (batter i, over o, wickets w) with the separable structure p_iowj ∝ τ_owj · p_i70j: a batter baseline at "over 7, 0 wickets" times a situation multiplier shared by all batters, estimated in two steps (τ by chained adjacent-state ratios, baselines by Dirichlet empirical-Bayes MCMC with prior sample size c=60). Bowlers get a symmetric q_iowj, and batter-vs-bowler probabilities are combined additively as p + q − p̄. Validated by Q-Q plots of team first-innings totals, a wickets-vs-overs curve, and agreement with sportsbook win probabilities for the 2014 World T20.

### Cited Findings

**Davis, Perera & Swartz (2015a) "A simulator for Twenty20 cricket", Australian & New Zealand Journal of Statistics 57(1):55-71** — author PDF: [t20sim.pdf](https://www.sfu.ca/~tswartz/papers/t20sim.pdf)

- Data: all T20 internationals between the 10 ICC full members from 17 Feb 2005 to 13 Nov 2013, 250 matches, parsed from ESPNcricinfo commentary with "a proprietary R-script"; 384 batsmen; 30,458 first-innings batting outcomes. Team first-innings means range from 129.9 (Zimbabwe) to 161.7 (Australia). — [t20sim.pdf](https://www.sfu.ca/~tswartz/papers/t20sim.pdf)
- Outcome space (eq. 1): j=0..6 runs scored (j=5 retained "to facilitate straightforward notation"), j=7 dismissal. Byes, leg byes and no-balls are folded in by their resultant runs; wides are excluded from the multinomial and generated separately. — [t20sim.pdf](https://www.sfu.ca/~tswartz/papers/t20sim.pdf)
- Observed T20 first-innings proportions (Table 2): p̂0..p̂7 = 0.301, 0.411, 0.078, 0.006, 0.103, 0.002, 0.042, 0.057. For comparison Test 4th-innings proportions (from Perera, Gill & Swartz): 0.743, 0.128, 0.035, 0.010, 0.065, 0.000, 0.003, 0.016 — sixes are 14× more frequent in T20. — [t20sim.pdf](https://www.sfu.ca/~tswartz/papers/t20sim.pdf)
- Extras: in the simulator an extra occurs with probability q1 = 0.033 per delivery, adds one run, and the ball is not counted (return to start). (The 2015b/2016 papers quote 5.1% for extras when wides AND no-balls are both excluded from the multinomial.) — [t20sim.pdf](https://www.sfu.ca/~tswartz/papers/t20sim.pdf); [moneyball.pdf](https://www.sfu.ca/~tswartz/papers/moneyball.pdf)
- Exploratory finding motivating situational structure: the proportion of 4s is very low in overs 1–2, spikes during the powerplay (overs 1–6), "plummets in the 7th over", then increases nearly monotonically to over 20; batting also depends on wickets lost and on the over×wickets interaction ("a batsman is more aggressive in the 19th over with two wickets lost than in the 19th over with 9 wickets lost"). Teams were all out in only 11% of first innings. — [t20sim.pdf](https://www.sfu.ca/~tswartz/papers/t20sim.pdf)
- Likelihood (eq. 2): (X_iow0..X_iow7) ~ Multinomial(m_iow; p_iow0..p_iow7), X_iowj = count of outcome j by batter i in over o with w wickets down. — [t20sim.pdf](https://www.sfu.ca/~tswartz/papers/t20sim.pdf)
- Core simplification (eq. 3): p_iowj = τ_owj · p_i70j / Σ_j τ_owj · p_i70j. p_i70j is batter i's baseline "immediately following the powerplay (i.e. the 7th over) when no wickets have been taken"; τ_owj is a multiplicative situation factor common to all batters ("an indicator of aggression"; when aggression rises τ_ow4 > 1 and τ_ow6 > 1). Parameter count: (19)(9)(7) τ's + (384)(7) baselines = 3,885, data:parameter ratio ≈ 8:1. Joint MCMC "unable ... to obtain a convergent Markov chain", so a two-step profile-likelihood-style procedure is used. — [t20sim.pdf](https://www.sfu.ca/~tswartz/papers/t20sim.pdf)
- Step 1 — estimating τ_owj (Appendix): for each batter compute empirical p̂_iowj = x_iowj / n_iow; define adjacent-over transition α̃_iowj = p̂_i,o+1,w,j / p̂_iowj; average across batters with inverse-SD weights, α̂_owj = Σ_i v_iowj^(−1/2) α̃_iowj / Σ_i v_iowj^(−1/2), where by the delta method v_iowj = α̃²_iowj [ (1−p̂_i,o+1,w,j)/(n_i,o+1,w p̂_i,o+1,w,j) + (1−p̂_iowj)/(n_iow p̂_iowj) ]. Analogously β̂_owj for the adjacent-wicket transition (o,w)→(o,w+1). The matrices are smoothed. τ_owj is then the product of the nearest α̂ and β̂ multipliers along the straight line from (o=1,w=0) to (o,w). Rationale: direct estimation from the baseline to distant states is impossible "since there are very few (if any) batsmen who have batted in both stages", but adjacent stages share batters. — [t20sim.pdf](https://www.sfu.ca/~tswartz/papers/t20sim.pdf)
- Step 2 — baselines p_i70j given τ: Dirichlet(a_0..a_7) prior on each batter's baseline vector; posterior (eq. 4) ∝ Π_{i,o,w} Π_j (τ_owj p_i70j / Σ_k τ_owk p_i70k)^{X_iowj} × Π_i Π_j p_i70j^{a_j−1}; it factors by batter; full conditional (eq. 5) ∝ p_i700^{ΣX_iow0 + a_0 − 1} ⋯ p_i707^{ΣX_iow7 + a_7 − 1} / Π_{o,w} (Σ_j τ_owj p_i70j)^{m_iow}, sampled by Metropolis-within-Gibbs with a Dirichlet proposal whose parameters are the numerator exponents. — [t20sim.pdf](https://www.sfu.ca/~tswartz/papers/t20sim.pdf)
- Empirical-Bayes hyperparameters: a_j = c · Σ_{i,o,w}(X_iowj/τ_owj) / Σ_{i,o,w,k}(X_iowk/τ_owk), i.e. c times the τ-adjusted population mean outcome vector; c is the prior sample size (prior SD ∝ 1/√(c+1)); c = 60 chosen, results "do not differ practically" for c ∈ (50,80). Motivation: >200 of 384 batsmen faced fewer than 40 balls. — [t20sim.pdf](https://www.sfu.ca/~tswartz/papers/t20sim.pdf)
- Current form: replace X_iowj by Σ_g r^g X_iowjg with g = games before the last; decay r = 0.88 "via maximization of the posterior density". — [t20sim.pdf](https://www.sfu.ca/~tswartz/papers/t20sim.pdf)
- Bowler model (eq. 6): (Y_iow0..Y_iow7) ~ Multinomial(m_iow; q_iow0..q_iow7) for bowler i, estimated with the same empirical-Bayes machinery; q describes the bowler "with respect to an average batsman". — [t20sim.pdf](https://www.sfu.ca/~tswartz/papers/t20sim.pdf)
- Batter × bowler combination (eq. 7): for batter i1 facing bowler i2 use p_i1owj + q_i2owj − p̄_owj, where p̄_owj is the average batting characteristic (equal to the average bowling characteristic) for outcome j at stage (o,w). "If batsman i1 is average, then (7) reduces to q_i2owj, and if bowler i2 is average, then (7) reduces to p_i1owj." — [t20sim.pdf](https://www.sfu.ca/~tswartz/papers/t20sim.pdf)
- Home advantage: mean first-innings runs home 158.4 vs away 149.4; simulator multiplies each batter's runs by 2(158.4)/(158.4+149.4) = 1.03 at home and 0.97 away, rounding the total at the end; no adjustment at neutral venues. de Silva, Pond & Swartz (2001) estimated ODI home advantage ≈ 16 runs. — [t20sim.pdf](https://www.sfu.ca/~tswartz/papers/t20sim.pdf)
- Second innings (sec 4.3): let r1 = target, r2 = runs so far, d = D/L resources remaining (D/L table modified for T20). Required aggressiveness = (r1 − r2 + 1)/d (eq. 8). Batter's actual aggressiveness at stage (o,w): E1_iow = p1 + 2p2 + 3p3 + 4p4 + 6p6 (expected runs next ball) over E2_iow = x(p0+p1+p2+p3+p4+p6) + (x+y)p7 (expected resources consumed; x = resources for one ball, y = resources lost on a wicket, from the D/L table). If E1/E2 < (r1−r2+1)/d (eq. 9), find the minimum o* > o such that E1_io*w/E2_io*w ≥ (r1−r2+1)/d (eq. 10) and use p_io*wj; if none, use the o=20 characteristics (batter's personal maximum aggression). The authors call this the *optimal* behaviour and find real teams lag it. — [t20sim.pdf](https://www.sfu.ca/~tswartz/papers/t20sim.pdf)
- Validation: (a) Table 3 situational probabilities for an average batter / Watson / de Villiers at (over,wkts) = (7,0), (10,0), (15,2), (15,8): e.g. Average (7,0): 0 runs .3421, 1 .3700, 2/3 .0956, 4 .1212, 6 .0341, out .0370, E(RR) 7.5; Average (15,2): .2056, .4556, .0944, .0838, .0533, out .1072, E(RR) 7.8; Average (15,8): .2200, .4921, .0846, .0836, .0455, out .0742, E(RR) 7.6; Watson (7,0): .2804, .3794, .0868, .1046, .0935, out .0553, E(RR) 9.2; de Villiers (7,0): .3087, .4035, .1153, .1116, .0254, out .0356, E(RR) 7.4. (b) Q-Q plots of 1000 simulated vs actual first-innings totals for each ICC team over two years "in line"; (c) average wickets lost vs overs completed, simulated vs actual, "reasonable agreement" and increasing wicket rate through the innings; (d) 2014 World T20 final: P(SL wins | SL bats first) = 0.46 vs 0.61 if India bats first under optimal second-innings adjustment; multiplying the RHS of eq. 9 by 0.8 (teams only accelerate when 20% behind) gives 0.51/0.55, closer to the literature's "at most a minor advantage" for batting second; (e) Table 4: simulator vs sportsbook win probabilities for 15 WT20 2014 matches (e.g. India–Pakistan 0.54 vs 0.59; Pakistan–Australia 0.51 vs 0.35; SL–India final 0.53 vs 0.42); hypothetical $100 on each simulator-favoured underdog returned +$399 with 9/15 winners. — [t20sim.pdf](https://www.sfu.ca/~tswartz/papers/t20sim.pdf)
- Runtime: coded in R; "To simulate 1000 innings requires approximately 1.5 minutes of computation on an older laptop computer." Code not released. — [t20sim.pdf](https://www.sfu.ca/~tswartz/papers/t20sim.pdf)

**Davis, Perera & Swartz (2015b) "Player evaluation in Twenty20 cricket", Journal of Sports Analytics 1(1):19-31** — author PDF: [moneyball.pdf](https://www.sfu.ca/~tswartz/papers/moneyball.pdf)

- Data extended to 282 T20Is, Jan 2005 – Aug 2014; 35,356 first-innings balls; outcome 1 run is 41.1% (14,542); outcomes 5 and 3 are 0.2% (67) and 0.6% (214). Extras rate 5.1%. 448 players; 306 bowlers. — [moneyball.pdf](https://www.sfu.ca/~tswartz/papers/moneyball.pdf)
- Metric: expected run differential E(D(i)) = E(Ds(l_bat,i)) − E(Dc(l_bowl,i)) obtained by simulation, inserting player i into a "typical" lineup t_bat (position-k average of p_iowj over players whose mean batting position is k, positions 10–11 pooled) and t_bowl (five identical average bowlers each bowling 4 overs). Pure batsmen inserted at No. 3; all-rounders at No. 5; bowlers' 4 overs chosen uniformly at random. — [moneyball.pdf](https://www.sfu.ca/~tswartz/papers/moneyball.pdf)
- Results: best pure batsman Ahmed Shehzad E(D)=7.83; de Villiers 1.66; best bowler Senanayake 9.94; worst Mpofu −11.45; spread best–worst ≈ 13 runs (batsmen), 21 (bowlers), 13 (all-rounders). Correlation of E(D) with ICC ratings: 0.56 (batsmen), 0.61 (bowlers), −0.04 (all-rounders). Current-form vs career E(D) correlations 0.77/0.91/0.68. — [moneyball.pdf](https://www.sfu.ca/~tswartz/papers/moneyball.pdf)
- Published baseline vectors (Table 5; p_i700, p_i701, p_i702-3, p_i704, p_i706, p_i70w, E(R)/over): AB de Villiers career .305 .389 .109 .136 .028 .033 7.97; current .294 .367 .099 .176 .036 .029 8.96. M. Hafeez bat career .368 .365 .091 .113 .027 .037 7.02. U. Gul bowl career .400 .322 .098 .117 .022 .041 6.78; current .340 .306 .127 .153 .037 .037 8.44. M. Hafeez bowl career .349 .422 .084 .090 .029 .026 6.86. — [moneyball.pdf](https://www.sfu.ca/~tswartz/papers/moneyball.pdf)
- First- vs both-innings estimation of E(D) correlate r = 0.73; authors keep first-innings-only as primary because second-innings behaviour depends on the target. — [moneyball.pdf](https://www.sfu.ca/~tswartz/papers/moneyball.pdf)

### Inferences
- For a game engine the reusable skeleton is: (i) a per-player 6- or 7-vector baseline (0,1,2/3,4,6,out) at a reference state; (ii) a shared (over × wickets) multiplier table τ; (iii) renormalise; (iv) blend batter and bowler with p + q − p̄ (clip to [0,1] and renormalise — the paper does not say how it handles negative values, so this must be added); (v) a separate extras Bernoulli (~3.3% wides-only or ~5.1% all extras in T20I 2005–14); (vi) a chase-aggression rule that moves the batter to a later-over column of τ. This reproduces the published behaviour without MCMC if baselines are set by hand or by shrunk empirical frequencies with pseudo-count 60.
- Table 3 gives a ready-made sanity target for an engine: an average T20I batter (2005–13) should produce ≈7.5 runs/over at 3.7% dismissal rate after the powerplay, rising to ≈7.8 runs/over and ≈10.7% dismissal at over 15 with 2 down.
- Reading order for an engine designer: optorders.pdf (simplest full spec, 12 pp) → t20sim.pdf (production structure, Appendix for τ) → Brooker thesis ch. 5 (state-value calibration) → Stevenson & Brewer 2017 (if you want a within-innings "getting set" hazard for Tests).

### Gaps
- τ_owj values are not tabulated anywhere (only described); nor are the smoothing method or the "straight line" path rule in detail.
- No formal calibration metric (log-loss, Brier, chi-square) is reported in any SFU paper; validation is graphical plus sportsbook agreement.
- No code released for any of the SFU simulators; the commentary parser is "proprietary".

## Q3. Perera, Davis & Swartz: fielding impact, optimal lineups (2016), bowler selection / bowling order

### Takeaway
Fielding is modelled as a per-fielder 8×8 row-stochastic "conversion" matrix Λ applied to the batting outcome vector (p* = Λᵀp), estimated from commentary text via random forests; best fielders save ~1.2 runs/match vs typical, with the fielder-average E(RSF) = −1.20 (because mistakes are easier than brilliance). Optimal lineups jointly optimise selection, batting order and over-by-over bowling order by simulated annealing on simulated run differential; bowling order is modelled as a 20-slot sequence from a 4·M_B multiset with no consecutive overs by the same bowler.

### Cited Findings

**Perera, Davis & Swartz, "Assessing the impact of fielding in Twenty20 cricket"** (ANZJS 2018; author PDF) — [fielding.pdf](https://www.sfu.ca/~tswartz/papers/fielding.pdf)

- Baseline: typical batting lineup vs typical bowling lineup simulates E(R) = 149.9 first-innings runs. Metric E(RSF) = E(R) − E(R*) where R* uses p*_k = Σ_j λ_jk p_j, i.e. p* = Λᵀp, with λ_jk = P(fielder converts standard outcome j into actual outcome k); rows sum to 1, diagonal near 1. Impossible conversions constrained to zero: λ_06 = λ_16 = λ_26 = λ_46 = λ_60 = 0, rows rescaled. — [fielding.pdf](https://www.sfu.ca/~tswartz/papers/fielding.pdf)
- Data: 286 T20Is (17 Feb 2005 – 3 Apr 2014) + 324 IPL matches (2009–2015) = 160,247 commentary lines; 146,452 with no fielder named (set A), 13,796 with a fielder named (set B). — [fielding.pdf](https://www.sfu.ca/~tswartz/papers/fielding.pdf)
- Estimation: random forest (R randomForest; 2,500 trees, min leaf 5, mtry 15; trained on 100,000 lines of A, rest for tuning) with 55 binary keyword covariates (Table 1 lists them: "across, air, almost, angle, back, backward, banged, bat, boundary, butt, charge, chipped, clip, cut, deep, delivery, drill, drive, easy, edge, fine, firm, flash, flick, foot, forward, front, gap, gloves, hard, high, hit, knee, leg, length, long, low, middle, midwicket, pads, pace, power, pull, reverse, short, side, slog, slower, square, strike, stump, sweep, swing, swung") predicting the outcome distribution q_i0..q_i7 a line "would have" produced. For a fielder on field for n balls and named in m lines with realised outcomes O_i: λ̂_kk = (n−m)/n + (m/n)·Σ_i I(O_i=k) q_ik / Σ_i q_ik and λ̂_jk = (m/n)·Σ_i I(O_i=k) q_ij / Σ_i q_ij (j≠k). Good/bad plays truncate the RF distribution to outcomes no better/worse than realised. Restricted to 157 players with ≥2,000 balls fielded. — [fielding.pdf](https://www.sfu.ca/~tswartz/papers/fielding.pdf)
- Example Λ for MS Dhoni (rows j = 0,1,2-3,4-5,6,7; cols same): diag .983/.981/.978/.982/.976/.982; largest off-diagonal λ_70 = 0.008 (dropped catches / missed stumpings). — [fielding.pdf](https://www.sfu.ca/~tswartz/papers/fielding.pdf)
- Results: wicketkeepers range from Mushfiqur Rahim +0.22 to Boucher −4.28 (Dhoni −3.61, n=15,980, m=467); non-keepers best Coulter-Nile +0.35; mean over 144 non-keepers −1.20, minimum −4.03 (Brett Lee); de Villiers −0.34 (21st). "The best fielders save on average 1.2 runs per match compared to a typical fielder." Negative skew because "there are more opportunities to make mistakes than to do something exceptional". **Perera, Davis & Swartz (2016) "Optimal lineups in Twenty20 cricket", Journal of Statistical Computation and Simulation** — author PDF: [lineups.pdf](https://www.sfu.ca/~tswartz/papers/lineups.pdf)

- Objective: E(D) = E(Rs) − E(Ra) against an average opponent, both simulated as *first* innings; N = 25,000 pairs of innings gives SE < 0.2 runs. Data 282 T20Is to 21 May 2014. — [lineups.pdf](https://www.sfu.ca/~tswartz/papers/lineups.pdf)
- Search space upper bound: C(M,11)·11!·11^20 = M!·11^20/(M−11)!; for M=15 ≈ 3.67×10^31. Simulated annealing: each step picks with prob 1/3 a team-selection swap (must keep ≥5 bowlers and a keeper), batting-order adjacent swap, or bowling-order swap within an ordered list of 4·M_B bowler symbols where the first 20 entries are the overs bowled (enforces 4-over cap; consecutive overs by same bowler disallowed); double swaps for i ≤ 500; cooling t_i = 0.5·(0.9)^⌊i/100⌋ with plateau exit after 20 acceptances; N_i = min(25000, 1000 + 16i); 1500 iterations ≈ 24 h on a 4-core laptop (R snow/snowfall). — [lineups.pdf](https://www.sfu.ca/~tswartz/papers/lineups.pdf)
- Bowler effect inside the simulator: same p + q − p̄ synthesis as DPS 2015a (cited, not re-derived). Players' characteristics can be hand-edited ("lowering his value of p_i707"). — [lineups.pdf](https://www.sfu.ca/~tswartz/papers/lineups.pdf)
- Results: India optimal E(D)=32.4 vs actual WC lineups 2.9/7.4/1.1 (optimal has only one fast bowler, Dhoni at No. 9); South Africa optimal 36.6 vs 14.7/19.8/14.7; all-star XI E(D)=61.6. Changes of <0.5 runs are treated as not meaningful. — [lineups.pdf](https://www.sfu.ca/~tswartz/papers/lineups.pdf)
- Earlier exploratory tables: batting strike rate vs average and economy vs bowling average form continua "with no apparent clustering", so selection should not fill role quotas. — [lineups.pdf](https://www.sfu.ca/~tswartz/papers/lineups.pdf)

**Related SFU bowler work**
- Epasinghege Dona, Nguyen, Gill & Swartz, "expected economy rate" framework: estimates batting outcome probabilities per ball with machine learning (random forests) given detailed ball data, to evaluate bowlers. — [xeconrate.pdf](https://www.sfu.ca/~tswartz/papers/xeconrate.pdf) (located via search; not read in full)

### Inferences
- The fielding matrix is a clean plug-in for a game: an engine can carry a per-team or per-fielder Λ (default identity) and post-multiply the batter×bowler outcome vector; realistic magnitudes are ±0.5–1% mass moved per row, worth ~1 run per match per elite/poor fielder.
- The lineups paper is the only academic treatment of bowling *order*; its multiset-of-4-symbols representation is a convenient data structure for an engine's bowling-plan optimiser.

### Gaps
- No paper in this group models *when* a specific bowler should bowl beyond brute-force SA; no bowler fatigue, spell length, or new-ball effect is estimated.
- Fielding Λ estimates have no uncertainty quantification (authors acknowledge).

## Q4. Duckworth-Lewis-Stern and WASP (Hogan & Brooker): expected runs vs overs remaining and wickets in hand, and usability for calibration

### Takeaway
D/L's published functional form is Z(u,w) = Z0(w)(1 − e^{−b(w)u}); official parameters are secret but a reverse-fitted parameter table exists, and the Standard Edition resource table is public. WASP (Brooker & Hogan, Canterbury) is a ball-by-ball backward-induction DP over (ball, wickets) using ordered-probit runs models and probit wicket models fitted to NZC ball-by-ball data for 311 top-8 ODIs; Brooker's thesis publishes every regression coefficient, so a first-innings expected-runs table and per-ball outcome distributions can be reconstructed.

### Cited Findings

**Duckworth-Lewis(-Stern)**
- Form: Z(u,w) = Z0(w)·[1 − exp(−b(w)·u)], u = overs remaining, w = wickets lost; Z0(w) = asymptotic average total in unlimited overs, b(w) = decay constant; both vary with w only. Resource fraction P(u,w) = Z(u,w)/Z(50,0). The 1998 Duckworth & Lewis paper uses the alternative parameterisation Z(u,w) = Z0·F(w)·[1 − exp(−b·u/F(w))] with F(0)=1. — [Wikipedia DLS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Duckworth%E2%80%93Lewis%E2%80%93Stern_method)
- Official Z0(w), b(w) "were not disclosed due to commercial confidentiality". Standard Edition is a single public table (hand-usable; target raised by G50·(R2−R1)/100 when team 2 has more resources, G50 = average 50-over first-innings score); Professional Edition (ICC internationals since 2004) uses a different table per team-1 total and proportional targets S·R2/R1; its values are not public. — [Wikipedia DLS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Duckworth%E2%80%93Lewis%E2%80%93Stern_method)
- Standard Edition excerpts (% resources remaining): 50 overs/10 wkts in hand 100.0; 20 overs/10 wkts 56.6; 20 overs/2 wkts in hand 11.9; 10 overs/10 wkts 32.1; 10 overs/2 wkts 11.4. — [Wikipedia DLS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Duckworth%E2%80%93Lewis%E2%80%93Stern_method)
- Stern's refinement (2014/2015 adoption) adjusts the curve for modern scoring, especially T20, and recognises that chasing large targets needs a higher scoring rate; Wikipedia gives no equations. Stevenson & Brewer cite "Stern, 2016" for the published version (JORS). — [Wikipedia DLS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Duckworth%E2%80%93Lewis%E2%80%93Stern_method); [1908.11490](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1908.11490)
- Reverse-engineered parameters (fitted by a blogger to the public D/L tables with Desmos; NOT official): w=0: Z0=134.10, b=0.02739; w=1: 118.53, 0.03100; w=2: 101.91, 0.03604; w=3: 84.45, 0.04350; w=4: 67.00, 0.05487; w=5: 50.28, 0.07308; w=6: 35.12, 0.10462; w=7: 21.99, 0.16721; w=8: 11.91, 0.30987; w=9: 4.70, 0.76322. — [Flying Colours Maths blog](https://blog.flyingcoloursmaths.co.uk/red-rag-duckworth-lewis-stern)
- Davis et al. (2015a) use "the Duckworth-Lewis table modified for T20" for resources d, per-ball resource cost x and per-wicket cost y in their chase model. — [t20sim.pdf](https://www.sfu.ca/~tswartz/papers/t20sim.pdf)

**WASP**
- WASP was developed by Scott Brooker (PhD) with supervisor Seamus Hogan at the University of Canterbury; since 2018 owned by NV Play. First-innings model: additional runs expected as a function of balls and wickets remaining; second-innings model: P(win) as a function of balls and wickets remaining, runs scored and target; estimates "obtained using a dynamic programme rather than just curve fitting". Hogan: it answers "if the average team batting against the average bowling team on this pitch was to do its average performance from this point on, what would the outcome be?" — [Wikipedia WASP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WASP_(cricket_calculation_tool)); [NZC "What's WASP all about"](https://www.nzc.nz/news-items/archive/what-s-wasp-all-about)
- Critique: the Markov assumption behind a DP like WASP yields thin tails, so a six or a wicket can swing the estimate sharply. (Single blogger.) — [noenthuda.com](https://www.noenthuda.com/2014/03/13/wasps-have-thin-tails-or-why-cricket-prediction-algorithms-fail)

**Brooker (2010) PhD thesis "An economic analysis of ability, strategy and fairness in ODI cricket", Univ. of Canterbury (244 pp)** — [PDF mirror](https://www.cmi.ac.in/~rlk/Cricket_Analytics/AuxiliaryMaterial/AnEconomicAnalysisofAbilityStrategyandFairnessinODICricket.pdf)
- Data: ball-by-ball for 311 ODIs, 20 Jul 2001 – 25 Jan 2008, collected by New Zealand Cricket, all between top-8 nations; 185 pre-powerplay-rule and 126 powerplay-era matches; ≈91,000 legal-ball observations. — [Brooker thesis](https://www.cmi.ac.in/~rlk/Cricket_Analytics/AuxiliaryMaterial/AnEconomicAnalysisofAbilityStrategyandFairnessinODICricket.pdf)
- First-innings state: i = next ball (1..300, 301 terminal), j = wickets lost (0..10); ground conditions θ enter as a parameter, not a state. Assumes P(win) linear in first-innings score (verified by smoothed win% vs score, 41-point window) so the objective is expected additional runs V(i,j); assumes no serial correlation between balls. — [Brooker thesis](https://www.cmi.ac.in/~rlk/Cricket_Analytics/AuxiliaryMaterial/AnEconomicAnalysisofAbilityStrategyandFairnessinODICricket.pdf)
- Bellman equation (eq. 19): V(i,j) = E[r_ij] + ω_ij·V(i+1, j+1) + (1−ω_ij)·V(i+1, j) + λ_ij·E[ε_ij]/(1−λ_ij), with V(301,j)=0, V(i,10)=0; r_ij = runs off a legal ball, ω_ij = P(wicket), λ_ij = P(wide/no-ball), ε_ij ∈ {1,2,..,7} runs from an extra; the geometric-series term handles repeated extras; dismissals on extras are reassigned to the legal ball. State space 3,311 cells. — [Brooker thesis](https://www.cmi.ac.in/~rlk/Cricket_Analytics/AuxiliaryMaterial/AnEconomicAnalysisofAbilityStrategyandFairnessinODICricket.pdf)
- Per-ball outcome model: ordered probit on r ∈ {0,1,2,3,4,6} ("gives the probability of r taking each value"), fitted separately for the fielding-restriction period (balls ≤90 pre-2005, ≤120 in powerplay era) and after; covariates: ball i, wicket dummies W_0..W_9, interactions W_k·i, i2 = max(0, i−48) (new-ball/settling kink), i3 = max(0, i−240) (death-overs kink), powerplay-era dummy pp, and optionally conditions θ. Wicket probability: probit on same covariates without W·i interactions. Wides/no-balls: single probit, decreasing in i (coef −0.0013). Runs from an extra: ordered probit on {1,2,3,4,5,7}, mean ≈1 early rising to ≈1.7 at the death. Observation counts by wickets (with/without restrictions): 0: 11390/903; 1: 9988/5285; 2: 6396/8329; 3: 2826/11734; 4: 813/11607; 5: 323/7998; 6: 33/5861; 7: 0/4260; 8: 0/2245; 9: 0/1246. — [Brooker thesis](https://www.cmi.ac.in/~rlk/Cricket_Analytics/AuxiliaryMaterial/AnEconomicAnalysisofAbilityStrategyandFairnessinODICricket.pdf)
- Published coefficients (Appendix C, "without conditions" columns). Wickets, with restrictions: intercept −2.0799; W0 0.0645, W1 −0.0544, W2 −0.1072, W3 −0.1028; i 0.00244; i2 −0.00305. Wickets, without restrictions: intercept −2.2046; W1 −0.0649 … W9 −0.0132 (W4 −0.1858, W7 −0.1691); i 0.0014; i3 0.0102. P(wide/no-ball): intercept −1.6905, i −0.0013. - Thin-data fixes: compute survival probability S(i,j) to ball 300 by backward induction (eq. 20); where S < 0.1 freeze the runs/wicket functions at the last such i; for late innings with few wickets down, linearly interpolate from (240, j) to the data-rich cell (300, 4) for j = 0..3. — [Brooker thesis](https://www.cmi.ac.in/~rlk/Cricket_Analytics/AuxiliaryMaterial/AnEconomicAnalysisofAbilityStrategyandFairnessinODICricket.pdf)
- Fit: V-functions vs observed average additional runs (cells with ≥30 obs) — root weighted MSE by wickets (non-powerplay era): j=0 4.29, 1 7.23, 2 4.37, 3 4.26, 4 3.39, 5 3.53, 6 1.75, 7 1.07, 8 0.66 runs; worst cell ≈3% of average first-innings total. — [Brooker thesis](https://www.cmi.ac.in/~rlk/Cricket_Analytics/AuxiliaryMaterial/AnEconomicAnalysisofAbilityStrategyandFairnessinODICricket.pdf)
- Conditions: a latent ground-conditions variable θ inferred from first-innings score and result (normal conditional distributions), injected by simulating 100 draws per ball and weighting (SAS); adds θ terms to all four probits (runs with-restrictions θ 0.00155; wickets without-restrictions θ −0.00483 and θ·i 0.000014). — [Brooker thesis](https://www.cmi.ac.in/~rlk/Cricket_Analytics/AuxiliaryMaterial/AnEconomicAnalysisofAbilityStrategyandFairnessinODICricket.pdf)
- Second innings (ch. 7): state adds k = runs still required; π(i,j,k) = P(team 2 wins) with Bellman eq. 25 over outcomes r (runs) and wickets; required run rate k* = k/(301−i) used as a regressor; data filtered to competitive states (e.g. 0.6 ≤ k* ≤ 1.95 for i ≤ 240; 0.5 ≤ k* ≤ 1.65 for 120 ≤ i ≤ 239; k* ≤ 1.35 for i ≤ 119) because hopeless/trivial chases "contaminate" behaviour; Appendix D holds the second-innings coefficients. — [Brooker thesis](https://www.cmi.ac.in/~rlk/Cricket_Analytics/AuxiliaryMaterial/AnEconomicAnalysisofAbilityStrategyandFairnessinODICricket.pdf)

**Independent modern re-implementations of the same idea**
- Mishra (2026), arXiv 2608.14696: exact backward-induction win-probability over (balls remaining, wickets lost, runs required) for T20 chases from a single per-ball outcome distribution over {0..6, wicket}; marginals match data within TV ≤ 0.02 at every required rate, yet win probability is systematically miscalibrated; the error is attributed to 3–5-ball run-scoring persistence (innings-level heterogeneity only ≈18%; wickets slightly anti-clustered); a block-bootstrap simulator closes 26% of the gap, saturating at ~20-ball blocks. No code release. — [arXiv 2608.14696](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.14696)
- Bandyopadhyay (2026), arXiv 2608.18020: on 2,727,420 legal T20 deliveries (12,492 matches, 19 competitions, Feb 2005 – Jun 2026, baseline fields from Cricsheet) fits a multiplicative log-linear expected-runs model μ(x) = μ0·γ_scen(phase,innings)·γ_era(year,phase)·γ_wkt(phase,wickets)·γ_type(phase,pace/spin subtype)·γ_venue(ground,3-yr bucket)·γ_opp(bowler id), each cell multiplier = Σruns/Σfitted (conditional Poisson MLE) by iterated backfitting with empirical-Bayes shrinkage; deviance drops when a factor is removed: venue 41,767 (1,267 df), wickets 27,972 (32 df), opposition bowler 27,545, scenario 21,686, era 8,611, bowler type 2,812; on the 804k-ball tracked subset, length adds 41,924 and line 3,409. Also solves a Bellman *expectation* value function V_e(m,w,i,j) over balls remaining m, wickets in hand w, striker tier i, non-striker tier j (6 quality tiers by shrunk scoring rate), with per-(tier,phase,innings) hazard h and scoring distribution π over {0..6} shrunk by Dirichlet/Beta pseudo-counts, strike rotation on odd runs/over end, incoming batter = empirical average tier for that position. Code and files "will be made publicly available in the author's GitHub repository" (metrics proprietary to an app). — [arXiv 2608.18020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.18020)
- Patel/"Markov Cricket" (2021), arXiv 2103.04349: ODI modelled as a Markov process; Monte-Carlo-fitted nonlinear value function per state claimed to beat DLS "by 3 to 10 times" as a proxy for remaining resources; inverse RL (guided cost learning) infers a linear reward from winning teams' play and a simulator samples final-score posteriors. Only the abstract was readable; data source and code not confirmed. — [arXiv 2103.04349](https://arxiv.org/abs/2103.04349)

### Inferences
- D/L resource tables are a legitimate calibration target: a simulator's mean remaining runs E[V(u,w)] should be proportional to P(u,w); the reverse-fitted (Z0,b) table reproduces the Standard Edition closely and can be used as a smooth check-function, but should be labelled unofficial.
- Brooker's Appendix C coefficients allow a complete first-innings ODI per-ball generator (ordered probit → outcome probabilities; probit → wicket; probit → extra; ordered probit → extra runs) for 2001–2008 ODI scoring levels without any new data. Scoring rates have since risen (DLS was revised for this), so an era multiplier is needed.
- The two 2026 arXiv papers show that a state-only Markov chain matches per-ball marginals but under-disperses totals; a game simulator that wants realistic collapses/surges should add short-range momentum (≈3–5 balls) or innings-level random effects (pitch/day), echoing Mishra's finding.

### Gaps
- Stern's 2016 JORS paper (exact DLS modification and T20 parameters) was not retrieved; only secondary descriptions.
- WASP's production model (post-2011, NV Play) is proprietary; only the thesis-era method is documented.
- Brooker's thesis tables of V(i,j) values are figures, not numbers; the engine would need to recompute V from the coefficients.

## Q5. Hierarchical Bayesian / hazard models from other authors (Kimber & Hansford 1993; Brewer 2008; Stevenson & Brewer 2017, 2021; Scarf, Shi & Akhtar 2011; others)

### Takeaway
The Auckland line (Brewer; Stevenson & Brewer) models a Test batter's dismissal hazard per run scored as H(x) = 1/(μ(x)+1) with an "effective average" that rises exponentially from μ1 (fresh) to μ2 (set) with e-folding scale L; posterior estimates for named players show batters start at roughly a quarter to a half of their set ability and get set within ~2–6 runs. The 2021 extension puts a powered-exponential Gaussian process on log μ2 across career innings and adds multiplicative home/away (ψ) and first/second team-innings (φ) effects. Kimber & Hansford (1993) is the nonparametric ancestor; Scarf, Shi & Akhtar (2011) model Test partnership/innings totals with negative binomials using run rate as a strategy covariate.

### Cited Findings

**Kimber & Hansford (1993) JRSS-A 156(3):443-455** — [RePEc record](https://ideas.repec.org/a/bla/jorssa/v156y1993i3p443-455.html)
- Abstract: the traditional batting average "depends on an unrealistic parametric assumption" (geometric scores); they propose a nonparametric (product-limit / survival-analysis) estimator of the hazard of dismissal at each score, treating not-outs as censored; applied to a large sample of players. — [RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/bla/jorssa/v156y1993i3p443-455.html)
- Later authors: K&H found batters more likely to be dismissed at low scores (support for "getting your eye in") but the nonparametric hazard "jumps erratically" at high scores due to sparsity; Cai, Hyndman & Wand (2002) smoothed it. — [arXiv 1609.04078](https://arxiv.org/abs/1609.04078)

**Brewer (2008) "Getting your eye in", arXiv:0801.4408** — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/0801.4408)
- Likelihood: P(X=x) = H(x) Π_{a<x}[1−H(a)]; not-outs contribute G(y) = Π_{a<y}[1−H(a)]. Hazard via effective average H(x) = 1/(μ(x)+1); logistic change-point μ(x) = μ1 + (μ2−μ1)/(1+exp(−(x−τ)/L)). Priors: μ1, μ2 ~ N(30, 20²) truncated >0; τ ~ Exp(mean 20); L ~ Exp(mean 3). Metropolis-Hastings; evidence by annealed importance sampling. "C++ source code and data files ... will be provided by the author on request." — [arXiv 0801.4408](https://arxiv.org/abs/0801.4408)
- Estimates (posterior mean ± sd; μ1, μ2, τ, L): Lara 14.5±8.3, 60.2±4.7, 5.1±2.9, 2.8±1.8; S. Waugh 10.5±5.5, 57.3±4.4, 1.8±1.6, 0.8±1.2; Langer 24.3±11.5, 49.6±4.9, 8.9±14.3, 2.8±2.9; Kirsten 16.6±9.3, 54.1±5.7; Hussain 15.6±9.1, 42.1±4.4; Cairns 26.9±9.2, 36.7±5.5; Pollock 22.1±7.7, 38.9±5.4; Warne 3.5±2.0, 21.1±2.0, 1.1±0.6, 0.5±0.4. log Bayes factors vs constant hazard 7.87–25.29 ("smallest ... still over 2500 to 1"). Conclusion: "a batsman is typically only playing at about half of their potential ability when they first arrive at the crease". — [arXiv 0801.4408](https://arxiv.org/abs/0801.4408)

**Stevenson & Brewer (2017) "Bayesian survival analysis of batsmen in Test cricket", JQAS 13(1):25-36, arXiv:1609.04078** — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/1609.04078)
- Model: μ(x; μ1, μ2, L) = μ2 + (μ1 − μ2)·exp(−x/L); H(x) = 1/(μ(x)+1); reparameterised μ1 = C·μ2, L = D·μ2 with C, D ∈ [0,1] so H(x) = 1/(μ2 + μ2(C−1)exp(−x/(Dμ2)) + 1). Priors: μ2 ~ LogNormal(log 25, 0.75²) (prior 68% CI [11.8, 52.9]); C ~ Beta(1,2); D ~ Beta(1,5). Log-likelihood eq. 6 (sum over outs of log H(x_i) + Σ_{a<x_i} log[1−H(a)] plus censored terms). Nested sampling (Julia, github.com/eggplantbren/NestedSampling.jl), 1000 particles, 1000 MCMC steps/iteration. Data from Statsguru via R package cricketr. — [arXiv 1609.04078](https://arxiv.org/abs/1609.04078)
- Individual estimates (median, 68% CI; μ1 / μ2 / L): Lara 15.1 [11.6,19.7] / 61.8 [56.7,67.5] / 6.1 [3.4,10.0]; S. Waugh 13.2 / 58.5 / 3.1; Kirsten 14.4 / 53.9 / 6.3; Langer 18.0 / 49.2 / 2.7; Hussain 12.8 / 40.8 / 1.9; Cairns 16.6 / 36.1 / 2.3; Pollock 18.2 / 37.4 / 5.6; Warne 5.3 / 21.2 / 1.3. log(Z/Z0) vs constant hazard: 1.04 (Cairns) to 15.60 (Warne). "Most players begin their innings playing with between only a quarter and half of their potential batting ability." — [arXiv 1609.04078](https://arxiv.org/abs/1609.04078)
- Hierarchical stage: μ2,i | ν,σ ~ LogNormal(ν, σ²), ν ~ U(1,100), σ ~ U(0,10), fitted by importance-reweighting individual posteriors (eq. 23). For 19 NZ openers since 1990 (546 innings): ν = 27.85 (+3.74/−3.55), σ = 0.54 (+0.11/−0.08); predicted next NZ opener μ1 = 9.6, μ2 = 27.7, L = 3.1. Richardson is the outlier (μ1 30.7, μ2 46.1). — [arXiv 1609.04078](https://arxiv.org/abs/1609.04078)
- Acknowledged confounders not modelled: pitch/light deterioration, bowler changes, new ball after 80 overs, breaks in play, milestones (nervous nineties). — [arXiv 1609.04078](https://arxiv.org/abs/1609.04078)

**Stevenson & Brewer (2021) "Finding your feet: a Gaussian process model for estimating the abilities of batsmen in Test cricket", JRSS-C 70(2):481-506, arXiv:1908.11490** (the "career trajectories" title is the 2018 BAYSM proceedings version, arXiv:1903.07218) — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/1908.11490)
- Within-innings as 2017. Between innings: μ(x,t) = [μ2t + (μ1 − μ2t)exp(−x/L)] × ψ^{v_t} × φ^{i_t}, with v_t ∈ {1 home, 0 neutral, −1 away}, i_t ∈ {1 team's first innings, −1 second}; log μ2t ~ GP(λ, K) with powered-exponential kernel K(t_j,t_k) = σ² exp(−|j−k|^α/ℓ^α), α ∈ [1,2] (α→1 Ornstein-Uhlenbeck/AR(1), α→2 squared exponential; SE was rejected as too smooth). Priors: log λ ~ N(log 25, 0.75²); log σ ~ N(log 0.2, 1²); log ℓ ~ N(log 20, 1²); α ~ U(1,2); log ψ, log φ ~ N(0, 0.25²); C ~ Beta(1,2), D ~ Beta(1,5). Fitted by a C++ nested sampler (1000 particles). Data: all 1,018 players who batted in a 21st-century Test, 40,273 innings; empirically averages are ≈17% higher at home than away and ≈20% higher in a team's first innings than its second. Claimed to predict better than batting average and ICC ratings (prediction-error section not read). No code link on arXiv. — [arXiv 1908.11490](https://arxiv.org/abs/1908.11490)

**Scarf, Shi & Akhtar (2011) "On the distribution of runs scored and batting strategy in test cricket", JRSS-A 174(2):471-497** — [Salford repository](https://salford-repository.worktribe.com/output/1445770/on-the-distribution-of-runs-scored-and-batting-strategy-in-test-cricket); [RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/bla/jorssa/v174y2011i2p471-497.html)
- Abstract: negative binomial distributions fitted to partnership scores and innings scores; for partnerships a parametric model with run rate as a covariate "to use run rate as a surrogate for batting strategy"; match-outcome probabilities from a model for outcome given end-of-third-innings position plus run-rate-dependent transition probabilities from the current position; lets a captain judge aggressive vs defensive approaches. Davis et al. summarise it as "a zero-inflated negative binomial distribution to each of the 10 partnerships". — [Salford](https://salford-repository.worktribe.com/output/1445770/on-the-distribution-of-runs-scored-and-batting-strategy-in-test-cricket); [t20sim.pdf](https://www.sfu.ca/~tswartz/papers/t20sim.pdf)

**Other relevant work surfaced**
- Koulis, Muthukumarana & Briercliffe (2014) JQAS 10(1):1-13, "A Bayesian stochastic model for batting performance evaluation in one-day cricket" — innings-to-innings form model (not per-ball). — [RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/bpj/jqsprt/v10y2014i1p1-13n2.html)
- Boys & Philipson, "On the ranking of Test match batsmen" (JRSS-C 2019; arXiv 1806.05496) — hierarchical model of Test batting; cited by Stevenson & Brewer as overcoming ICC-rating issues. Not read. — [arXiv 1806.05496](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1806.05496)
- Opta's "Next Ball Predictor" (industry): forecasts next-legal-delivery outcome probabilities using batter and bowler quality interactions, home/country advantage, venue tendencies, required run rate and balls faced by the batter; no model details published. — [The Analyst](https://theanalyst.com/articles/opta-next-ball-predictor)

### Inferences
- For a game, the exponential effective-average curve is a two-line addition: multiply a batter's base dismissal hazard by (μ_set+1)/(μ(x)+1) where μ(x) = μ_set + (μ_fresh − μ_set)e^{−x/L}; typical Test values μ_fresh ≈ 0.25–0.5 μ_set, L ≈ 2–6 runs. The same structure applies per *ball faced* in limited overs if x is replaced by balls (the authors suggest balls/minutes as better clocks).
- The GP paper's ψ/φ multipliers give empirical magnitudes for home advantage (+17% average) and pitch deterioration across a Test (−20% in the second team innings) that can directly scale per-ball dismissal probabilities in a Test engine.

### Gaps
- Kimber & Hansford full text not accessed (only abstract and citing summaries).
- Scarf, Shi & Akhtar's negative-binomial parameter estimates and covariate set were not retrievable (Salford PDF returned 403; RePEc has no full text).
- Stevenson & Brewer 2021 prediction-error numbers vs ICC ratings were not extracted.

## Q6. Test cricket simulation (innings length, declaration, draw probability, pitch deterioration)

### Takeaway
Perera, Gill & Swartz (2014, JQAS 10(1):15-26) give a fully specified, parameter-complete Test third/fourth-innings model: per-ball 8-outcome proportions for teams about to declare and for fourth innings, geometric (per-over) wicket-fall parameters r_1..r_10 for the fourth innings, and an order-constrained normal model for runs given overs used; from these they derive W/D/L probabilities and an optimal-declaration table. Scarf & Akhtar (2011, JORS) model Test outcomes by multinomial regression on end-of-innings positions (391 Tests). Pitch deterioration appears only as Stevenson & Brewer's innings effect (φ) and Brooker's latent conditions.

### Cited Findings

**Perera, Gill & Swartz (2014) "Declaration guidelines in test cricket", JQAS 10(1):15-26, doi 10.1515/jqas-2013-0118** — author PDF: [declare.pdf](https://www.sfu.ca/~tswartz/papers/declare.pdf)
- Data: 134 Tests with a 3rd-innings declaration (1 Mar 2001 – 31 Mar 2012, ICC nations), commentary parsed ball by ball; 1,849 innings for runs-vs-overs; 435 matches where a team batted 1st and 3rd. Declaring teams: 51% W, 2% L, 47% D. — [declare.pdf](https://www.sfu.ca/~tswartz/papers/declare.pdf)
- Per-ball proportions (p_−1 = dismissal, p_0..p_6): all 3rd-innings balls (62,948): .012 .664 .206 .040 .009 .065 .000 .005; last 10 overs before declaration (7,496): .027 .519 .293 .060 .007 .080 .000 .014; last 5 overs (3,659): .038 .490 .306 .063 .008 .079 .000 .016; fourth innings: .016 .743 .128 .035 .010 .065 .000 .003. Team-specific versions differed only minorly. — [declare.pdf](https://www.sfu.ca/~tswartz/papers/declare.pdf)
- Fourth-innings wickets: overs to the j-th wicket O_j ~ Geometric(r_j) (over-level Bernoulli), censored MLE r̂_j = l_j / Σ O_j; average team r_1..r_10 = .108 .079 .076 .081 .077 .094 .131 .166 .179 .212 (weak: .117 .085 .082 .087 .083 .102 .142 .179 .193 .228; strong: .099 .073 .070 .075 .071 .086 .120 .153 .165 .196); opening partnership falls faster than 2–5 ("adjustment period ... new ball"). — [declare.pdf](https://www.sfu.ca/~tswartz/papers/declare.pdf)
- Runs given overs: simple model R | i overs ~ N(3.02·i, 5.95²·i) judged too narrow; adopted order-constrained model R | i ~ N(μ_i, σ_i²) with μ_i, σ_i increasing in i, reference priors, Gibbs sampling: μ̂_50 = 169.6, μ̂_100 = 316.9, μ̂_150 = 508.6; σ̂_50 = 35.3, σ̂_100 = 55.1, σ̂_150 = 75.1 (posterior sds 3.7/5.5/7.0 and 1.2/3.0/5.8); not estimated beyond 180 overs. Strong/weak team = ±20% on μ_i. — [declare.pdf](https://www.sfu.ca/~tswartz/papers/declare.pdf)
- Decision rule: declare with y overs left if 2P(W|T_y;Q_y) + P(D|T_y;Q_y) > 2P(W|T_y;Q_y′) + P(D|T_y;Q_y′) (one more ball), or P(W) alone if a win is essential; P(W) = Σ_i q_i P(R_y < T | all out in i overs), P(D) = P(R ≤ T | not all out)·P(not all out in y). Optimal targets vs average opponent (criterion 1 / must-win): 50 overs 254/161; 90 overs 359/296; 120 overs 467/409; 150 overs 592/535; 180 overs 652/629. — [declare.pdf](https://www.sfu.ca/~tswartz/papers/declare.pdf)
- Validation: W/D/L table vs Scarf et al. shows "general agreement" (e.g. target 300 with 100 overs left: W .54 (.58), D .13 (.25), L .33 (.17)); out-of-sample 49 Tests (Apr 2012 – Apr 2013): rule never declared in the 36 matches where teams didn't; in 11 simulated declarations the rule declared slightly earlier in 6 of 7. — [declare.pdf](https://www.sfu.ca/~tswartz/papers/declare.pdf)

**Scarf & Akhtar (2011) "An analysis of strategy in the first three innings in test cricket: declaration and the follow-on", JORS 62(11):1931-1940** — [RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/pal/jorsoc/v62y2011i11p1931-1940.html)
- 391 Tests 1997–2007; outcome models on end-of-innings positions after innings 1, 2, 3; explanatory power rises from 44% (after 1st innings) to 80% (after 3rd); home advantage and team-strength effects shrink through the match while overs remaining and lead stay important; enforcing the follow-on has no effect on outcome. — [RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/pal/jorsoc/v62y2011i11p1931-1940.html)
- Scarf & Shi (2005, IMA J. Management Math 16:161-178) earlier timed the 3rd-innings declaration with multinomial logistic regression. — [declare.pdf](https://www.sfu.ca/~tswartz/papers/declare.pdf)

**Pitch deterioration / match-day effects**
- Stevenson & Brewer (2021): batting averages ≈20% higher in a team's first innings than its second; modelled by multiplier φ^{i_t}. — [arXiv 1908.11490](https://arxiv.org/abs/1908.11490)
- Brooker (2010) ODI: latent per-match "ground conditions" θ inferred from first-innings score and result; enters all per-ball probits. — [Brooker thesis](https://www.cmi.ac.in/~rlk/Cricket_Analytics/AuxiliaryMaterial/AnEconomicAnalysisofAbilityStrategyandFairnessinODICricket.pdf)
- Older Test simulators cited by Davis et al.: Elderton (1945) and Wood (1945) geometric scores; Dyte (1998) simulated Test batter-vs-bowler using career averages "without regard to the state of the match". — [t20sim.pdf](https://www.sfu.ca/~tswartz/papers/t20sim.pdf); [optorders.pdf](https://www.sfu.ca/~tswartz/papers/optorders.pdf)

### Inferences
- A Test engine can be built from PGS 2014 alone: per-ball multinomial (0.743/0.128/0.035/0.010/0.065/0/0.003/0.016 for normal batting; the 5-over-pre-declaration vector for slog mode), wicket hazard per over by partnership number (r_j above, ≈ r_j/6 per ball), and a draw clock of 90 overs/day. Overlay Stevenson–Brewer's eye-in hazard and a −20% second-team-innings multiplier for deterioration.
- No academic paper simulates a full five-day Test ball by ball with day-by-day pitch wear; the closest are PGS 2014 (3rd/4th innings only) and SSA 2011 (partnership totals).

### Gaps
- No found paper models over-rate / time-lost, weather interruptions, or follow-on as part of a simulator.
- Day-by-day deterioration is not estimated anywhere I found; only the first-vs-second team-innings contrast.

## Q7. Fatigue, pressure, pitch conditions, home advantage as covariates in ball-outcome models

### Takeaway
Within the academic per-ball literature these are thin: home advantage is handled by a crude runs multiplier (DPS 2015: ×1.03/×0.97) or an innings-level multiplier (S&B 2021: ψ ≈ +17%); pressure is proxied by required run rate (Brooker ch. 7; DPS chase rule via D/L resources; Bandyopadhyay via chase-state fields) rather than by a psychological variable; pitch/conditions appear as Brooker's latent θ and Bandyopadhyay's venue×3-year factor (the single largest-deviance factor). I found no peer-reviewed per-ball model with an explicit bowler-fatigue covariate.

### Cited Findings
- DPS 2015a: home factor 1.03/0.97 on runs; second-innings pressure via (r1−r2+1)/d resource ratio; 0.8 multiplier reproduces real teams' delayed acceleration. — [t20sim.pdf](https://www.sfu.ca/~tswartz/papers/t20sim.pdf)
- Brooker 2010: required run rate k* = k/(301−i) as regressor in second-innings ordered probits; data filtered to competitive states; new-ball kink at ball 48; "death" kink at ball 240; wides/no-balls most likely early ("new ball being harder to control"). — [Brooker thesis](https://www.cmi.ac.in/~rlk/Cricket_Analytics/AuxiliaryMaterial/AnEconomicAnalysisofAbilityStrategyandFairnessinODICricket.pdf)
- Bandyopadhyay 2026: deviance contributions venue 41,767 > wickets 27,972 > opposition bowler 27,545 > scenario 21,686 > era 8,611 > bowler type 2,812; length 41,924 and line 3,409 on tracked subset; second-innings value function estimated separately by innings "to reflect chase-state scoring", finer required-run-rate conditioning left as future work. — [arXiv 2608.18020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.18020)
- Stevenson & Brewer 2021: ψ (home/away) and φ (1st/2nd team innings) multipliers; empirical +17% home, +20% first innings. — [arXiv 1908.11490](https://arxiv.org/abs/1908.11490)
- Scarf & Akhtar 2011: home advantage and team strength effects on Test outcome decline as the match progresses. — [RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/pal/jorsoc/v62y2011i11p1931-1940.html)
- Perera, Gill & Swartz 2014: opening-partnership wicket rate exceeds partnerships 2–5 (new-ball/settling effect). — [declare.pdf](https://www.sfu.ca/~tswartz/papers/declare.pdf)
- Industry/grey: Opta's next-ball model uses home/country advantage, venue tendencies, required rate and balls faced; a PSL dataset on IEEE DataPort includes "bowler fatigue" and "situational pressure indicators" among 27 variables, but no fitted model with those covariates was found in a journal. — [The Analyst](https://theanalyst.com/articles/opta-next-ball-predictor); [IEEE DataPort](https://ieee-dataport.org/documents/extra-deliveries-wides-and-no-balls-t20-cricket)
- Mishra 2026: after conditioning on state, residual 3–5-ball persistence remains and innings-level heterogeneity explains ≈18% of the miscalibration — i.e. "momentum/pressure" exists in the data beyond the state variables. — [arXiv 2608.14696](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.14696)

### Inferences
- The defensible evidence-based covariate set for an engine is: over/phase, wickets lost, batter and bowler identity (or tier), bowler type × phase, venue/pitch factor, innings number, required run rate (chase), balls faced by the striker (eye-in), and era. Fatigue would have to be invented (no published estimate).

### Gaps
- No published per-ball coefficients for fatigue, spell length, or partnership duration.
- No paper quantifies "pressure" independently of required run rate.

## Q8. Reported validation and calibration numbers

### Takeaway
Validation in this literature is mostly graphical (Q-Q plots of innings totals, wickets-vs-overs curves) plus a handful of quantitative checks: Brooker's RWMSE of 0.7–7.2 runs for expected-additional-runs by wicket; DPS's agreement with sportsbooks and a 9/15 hypothetical betting record; PGS's out-of-sample declaration check; Mishra's TV ≤ 0.02 per-ball marginal match alongside a systematic win-probability miscalibration; Brewer/S&B's log Bayes factors of 1–25 for varying vs constant hazard. No paper reports per-ball log-loss or Brier score for its outcome distribution.

### Cited Findings
- Swartz et al. 2006: Q-Q of 71 actual vs 71,000 simulated India first innings "fairly good"; wicket histogram shapes agree; mean 250.1 runs under the real order. — [optorders.pdf](https://www.sfu.ca/~tswartz/papers/optorders.pdf)
- DPS 2015a: Q-Q per team (1000 sims); wickets-vs-overs lines agree; Table 4 simulator vs sportsbook within ~0.05 for 9 of 15 matches; hypothetical +$399 on $1,500 staked; 1000 innings in 1.5 min in R. — [t20sim.pdf](https://www.sfu.ca/~tswartz/papers/t20sim.pdf)
- DPS 2015b: E(D) correlations with batting avg/SR 0.50/0.57 (batsmen), bowling avg/econ 0.71/0.77 (bowlers); ICC rating correlations 0.56/0.61/−0.04. — [moneyball.pdf](https://www.sfu.ca/~tswartz/papers/moneyball.pdf)
- Brooker 2010: RWMSE of V(i,j) vs observed averages (cells ≥30 obs) by wickets 0..8: 4.29, 7.23, 4.37, 4.26, 3.39, 3.53, 1.75, 1.07, 0.66 (non-powerplay era); worst ≈3% of mean first-innings total. — [Brooker thesis](https://www.cmi.ac.in/~rlk/Cricket_Analytics/AuxiliaryMaterial/AnEconomicAnalysisofAbilityStrategyandFairnessinODICricket.pdf)
- PGS 2014: W/D/L vs Scarf et al. agree in most cells; 36/36 non-declaration matches correctly not triggered out of sample. — [declare.pdf](https://www.sfu.ca/~tswartz/papers/declare.pdf)
- Brewer 2008 / S&B 2017: log Bayes factors for varying hazard 7.9–25.3 and 1.0–15.6 respectively. — [arXiv 0801.4408](https://arxiv.org/abs/0801.4408); [arXiv 1609.04078](https://arxiv.org/abs/1609.04078)
- Mishra 2026: per-ball outcome marginals within TV 0.02 at every required run rate, but win probability "systematically miscalibrated"; block bootstrap closes 26% of the gap. — [arXiv 2608.14696](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.14696)
- Bandyopadhyay 2026: all six factors significant at p < 10^−300 on 2.73M balls; on tracked balls modified vs plain RAE per-ball rates correlate 0.984. — [arXiv 2608.18020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.18020)

### Inferences
- A reasonable acceptance test for a new engine, mirroring the literature: (1) Q-Q of simulated vs real innings totals per team/format; (2) mean wickets lost by over; (3) per-ball marginal outcome frequencies by phase within ~0.02 TV; (4) expected remaining runs by (overs, wickets) within ~5 runs of a D/L- or data-derived table; (5) distribution width — check the simulator is not under-dispersed (Mishra's warning).

### Gaps
- No published per-ball log-loss/Brier benchmarks exist to compare against; the first engine to compute them will have nothing academic to beat.
