# Stable per-player T20 ratings from ball-by-ball data: form windows, recency weighting, shrinkage strength, minimum samples, reliability

Scope note: this covers (a) form window / recency decay, (b) pseudo-count prior strength for Dirichlet / empirical-Bayes shrinkage of per-ball outcome vectors, (c) minimum balls before display, (d) how to measure year-to-year reliability, plus the opposition/situation, era, and ageing side-questions. Peer-review status is flagged on every source: **[PR]** = peer-reviewed journal, **[preprint]** = arXiv, **[blog]** = analyst blog / media. Formulas are quoted as implemented in the sources, with symbols kept close to the originals.

Sources that could not be read: Boundary Line (Medium) age articles and ESPNcricinfo stats pages returned HTTP 403; Baseball Prospectus legacy pages are behind a bot wall. Where only a search snippet was available this is stated.

## Key Question 1 — What do Davis, Perera & Swartz (2015) use for recency weighting (0.88) and prior sample size (c = 60), and how were they chosen and validated?

### Takeaway
Both numbers are confirmed and come from the SFU simulator paper: the Dirichlet prior strength c = 60 was chosen by "tinkering" and shown to be practically irrelevant across c in (50, 80); the per-match decay r = 0.88 was chosen by maximising the posterior density (an empirical-Bayes / marginal-likelihood style choice), not by out-of-sample prediction. Neither parameter was validated against held-out matches in the published text.

### Cited Findings
- Model structure (the thing being shrunk): batting outcome probabilities for batsman i, over o, wickets w, outcome j (j = 0 dot, 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7 = wicket) are factorised as `p_iowj = τ_owj · p_i70j / Σ_k τ_owk · p_i70k` (their eq. 3), where `p_i70j` is the batsman's baseline outcome vector in "over 7, zero wickets" (immediately after the powerplay) and `τ_owj` is a multiplicative situation factor shared by all batsmen. The paper states the implicit assumption is that "the stage of the first innings (overs completed and wickets lost) affects all batsmen in the same fashion", acting as "an indicator of aggression" — [Davis, Perera & Swartz, "A simulator for Twenty20 cricket", SFU PDF, ANZJS 57(1):55–71, 2015 **[PR]**](https://www.sfu.ca/~tswartz/papers/t20sim.pdf)
- Parameter count and data: N = 384 batsmen; (19)(9)(7) + (384)(7) = 3,885 unknown parameters against 30,458 batting outcomes, "roughly 8:1" data-to-parameter ratio. Estimation is two-step ("reminiscent of profile likelihood"): τ_owj first, then the baselines p_i70j given τ — [t20sim.pdf **[PR]**](https://www.sfu.ca/~tswartz/papers/t20sim.pdf)
- Prior: `(p_i700, …, p_i707) ~ Dirichlet(a_0, …, a_7)`. Empirical-Bayes hyperparameters: `a_j = c · Σ_{i,o,w}(X_iowj / τ_owj) / Σ_{i,o,w,k}(X_iowk / τ_owk)` for c > 0, i.e. the prior mean is the league-wide outcome vector after transforming every ball to the (o = 7, w = 0) baseline state, and c sets its strength. "The standard deviations of the prior parameters p_i70j are proportional to 1/√(c+1). Therefore, the tuning parameter c > 0 may be thought of as the prior sample size." — [t20sim.pdf **[PR]**](https://www.sfu.ca/~tswartz/papers/t20sim.pdf)
- Choice of c: "We have tinkered with various choices and have found that c = 60.0 provides realistic characteristics. Also, we have observed that the simulation results do not differ practically based on the selection of c ∈ (50, 80)." Rationale: "there are batsmen with limited batting histories … more than 200 batsmen have faced fewer than 40 balls. Only a handful of batsmen have faced more than 500 balls." (Figure 2 histogram of first-innings balls faced, 384 batsmen) — [t20sim.pdf **[PR]**](https://www.sfu.ca/~tswartz/papers/t20sim.pdf)
- Posterior computation: Metropolis-within-Gibbs with Dirichlet proposals whose parameters are the exponents in the numerator of the full conditional (their eq. 5); posterior means used as estimates — [t20sim.pdf **[PR]**](https://www.sfu.ca/~tswartz/papers/t20sim.pdf)
- Recency weighting (current form): "The natural way to do this is to weight the data with more weight given to recent observations (i.e. matches). Operationally, this was achieved by replacing X_iowj in (4) with Σ_g r^g X_iowjg where r is a decay ratio and the subscript g is the match index that denotes the number of games before the last game was played. We determined r = 0.88 via maximization of the posterior density." — [t20sim.pdf **[PR]**](https://www.sfu.ca/~tswartz/papers/t20sim.pdf)
- Bowler effect on the outcome vector (eq. 7): for batsman i1 facing bowler i2 the simulator uses `p_{i1,owj} + q_{i2,owj} − p̄_owj`, where q are the bowler's outcome probabilities "with respect to an average batsman", estimated "using the same empirical Bayes approach", and p̄_owj is the league average for that situation. "If batsman i1 is average, then (7) reduces to q_{i2,owj}, and if bowler i2 is average, then (7) reduces to p_{i1,owj}." — [t20sim.pdf **[PR]**](https://www.sfu.ca/~tswartz/papers/t20sim.pdf)
- Other fixed constants: extras occur with probability q1 = 0.033; home advantage implemented as run scaling 2(158.4)/(158.4+149.4) = 1.03 (home) and 0.97 (away) from average first-innings totals 158.4 vs 149.4 — [t20sim.pdf **[PR]**](https://www.sfu.ca/~tswartz/papers/t20sim.pdf)
- Companion paper (expected run differential, E(D)): data were 282 full-member T20 matches, January 2005 to August 2014, 35,356 first-innings balls, parsed from Cricinfo commentary; single is the most common outcome (14,542, 41.1%); 3s are 0.6% and 5s 0.2% of balls — [Davis, Perera & Swartz, "Player evaluation in Twenty20 cricket", SFU PDF, J. Sports Analytics 1(1), 2015 **[PR]**](https://www.sfu.ca/~tswartz/papers/moneyball.pdf)
- The companion paper reuses the simulator's current-form weighting and reports how far "current form" (recency-weighted, to Aug 2014) departs from career: "The correlations are 0.77 for pure batsmen, 0.91 for bowlers and 0.68 for all-rounders. This suggests that although performances change over time, the changes are not typically great." — [moneyball.pdf **[PR]**](https://www.sfu.ca/~tswartz/papers/moneyball.pdf)
- External validity reported: current-form E(D) vs Reliance ICC rating (5 Oct 2014): r = 0.56 for 50 pure batsmen, r = 0.61 for 60 bowlers, r = −0.04 for 25 all-rounders — [moneyball.pdf **[PR]**](https://www.sfu.ca/~tswartz/papers/moneyball.pdf)

### Inferences
- r = 0.88 per match implies a half-life of ln(0.5)/ln(0.88) ≈ 5.4 matches and a total effective weight of 1/(1−0.88) ≈ 8.3 matches — i.e. the DPS "current form" window is very short (roughly one T20 World Cup plus a bilateral series). It was chosen to maximise the posterior (fit) on a dataset of only 282 matches over nine years, so it is tuned for *describing* recent form in a simulator, not for *forecasting* a future tournament. Treat 0.88 as a lower bound on decay for forecasting use.
- Because the decay is per *match* rather than per *calendar month*, a player with a dense franchise schedule decays far faster in calendar time than an international-only player. Any implementation that mixes leagues needs to decide whether to decay per match or per month; DPS give no guidance.
- The prior strength c = 60 and the insensitivity band (50, 80) are for a 7-category outcome vector of a batter's *baseline* state after transforming all balls to that state. Because the transformation pools every ball a batter has faced (not per phase), c = 60 is a prior on the whole career vector, not per phase. A per-phase prior will need to be at least as large in pseudo-balls per phase to achieve the same regularisation.
- The eq. (7) additive batter+bowler combination is not a probability-preserving operation (it can go negative or exceed 1 for extreme pairs); DPS do not discuss clipping. A log-odds or multiplicative combination is safer in implementation.
- The 0.91 bowler vs 0.77 batter career/current-form correlation is the only cricket-specific evidence found that bowling outcome rates are more stable than batting ones over time (consistent with bowlers having more balls per match: 24 balls per match vs ~15–20 for a typical top-order batter).

### Gaps
- No sensitivity analysis of r (the decay) is reported; only the point estimate 0.88 and the method ("maximization of the posterior density"). No out-of-sample test of whether 0.88 predicts the next match better than, say, 0.95 or no decay.
- No reporting of what c maximises any predictive criterion — the (50, 80) band is about simulated *match totals* not differing, not about player ranking stability.
- The estimation of τ_owj (appendix) could not be read from the extracted text; the paper defers to its appendix.

## Key Question 2 — What do recent preprints (Bandyopadhyay 2026 RAE/DAR; Ganesh 2026 n/(n+50)) use for shrinkage, and do they report predictive validity by prior strength?

### Takeaway
Bandyopadhyay (arXiv 2608.18020, 2.7M legal deliveries) fits every shrinkage strength by empirical Bayes from the data (κ_f = Var(r)/(μ₀²·τ_f²)) and reports κ_μ = 60 for batter per-ball scoring rate, κ_η = 40 for dismissal hazard, κ_π = 200 for the Dirichlet pooling of the 7-outcome vector toward the phase mean; Ganesh (arXiv 2604.13861) uses a fixed λ = n/(n + 50) per phase with Laplace α = 1. Neither reports predictive validity as a function of prior strength, and neither uses any recency weighting.

### Cited Findings (Bandyopadhyay, "Context-adjusted Player Evaluation for Twenty20 Cricket", arXiv 2608.18020v2 **[preprint]**)
- Data: men's T20, February 2005 to May 2026 (data updated 5 June 2026); 12,492 matches, 19 competitions, 2,819,700 deliveries, 2,727,420 legal deliveries. IPL subset: 1,243 matches, 285,761 legal deliveries, 2008–2026. Ball-tracking (line/length) covers only 16.5% of legal deliveries; the headline RAE and DAR use only baseline fields — [arXiv 2608.18020 HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.18020v2)
- Expected runs per legal ball is multiplicative: `μ(x_b) = μ₀ · γ^scen · γ^era · γ^wkt · γ^type · γ^ven · γ^opp`, grand mean μ₀ = 1.2425 runs per legal ball. Factor cells: scenario = phase × innings (8 cells); era = year × phase (69 cells); wicket = phase × wickets fallen 0–10 (33 cells); bowler type = phase × pace/spin sub-type (21 cells); venue = ground × 3-year bucket (1,268 cells); opposition = opposing bowler identity for batter evaluation (7,282 cells) or opposing batter identity for bowler evaluation (9,740 cells) — [arXiv 2608.18020 HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.18020v2)
- Fitting: backfitting in the order scenario → era → wicket → bowler type → venue → opposition until convergence, each update `γ̂_c = Σ_{b∈c} r_b / Σ_{b∈c} e_b` (the conditional Poisson MLE); converged fit is order-independent. RAE_b = r_b − μ̂(x_b); aggregated as per-ball mean and total; SE = sd(RAE_b)/√n_i — [arXiv 2608.18020 HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.18020v2)
- Empirical-Bayes shrinkage of every context factor: `γ_c = (n_c·γ̂_c + κ_f)/(n_c + κ_f) = ω_c·γ̂_c + (1−ω_c)·1`, with `ω_c = n_c/(n_c + κ_f)`. Between-cell variance τ_f² by DerSimonian–Laird method of moments; sampling variance `v_c = Var(r)·n_c/(Σe_b)²` calibrated to the empirical run variance Var(r) = 2.578 (not Poisson). Pseudo-count: `κ_f = Var(r)/(μ₀²·τ_f²)` — [arXiv 2608.18020 HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.18020v2)
- Fitted κ_f by factor (Table 3): scenario τ² = 0.0093, κ = 180; era τ² = 0.0016, κ = 1,043; wicket τ² = 0.0034, κ = 485; bowler type τ² = 0.0013, κ = 1,249; venue τ² = 0.0054, κ = 311; opposition bowler τ² = 0.0061, κ = 273; opposition batter τ² = 0.0249, κ = 67. (The running text describes scenario, venue and opposition-batter κ as ≈1.5–1.9 × 10², which disagrees with the table values 180 / 311 / 67.) — [arXiv 2608.18020 HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.18020v2)
- Player-level rates used in the dynamic programme: `μ_i = (Σ_{b:i} r_b + κ_μ·μ₀)/(n_i + κ_μ)` and `η_i = (d_i + κ_η·η₀)/(n_i + κ_η)`. Supplement Table S1 gives **κ_μ = 60** (batter scoring rate), **κ_η = 40** (dismissal hazard η_i and h_{i,φ,e}), **κ_π = 200** (Dirichlet pseudo-count pooling the per-outcome vector π_{r|i,φ,e} toward its phase mean), strike-share clip s_i ∈ [0.25, 0.75]. Batters are split into Q = 6 quality tiers by ball-weighted quantiles of μ_i; scoring and hazard by (tier, phase, innings) shrink toward the (phase, innings) pool — [arXiv 2608.18020 HTML (offset 100000)](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.18020v2)
- Deviance (Table 2): every factor significant at p < 10⁻³⁰⁰; opposition bowler, venue and wicket contribute the most deviance — [arXiv 2608.18020 HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.18020v2)
- Dismissal cost (DAR): Bellman expectation recursion with M₀ = 120 legal balls, `V_e(m,w,i,j) = h·V_e(m−1,w−1,ι(w),j) + (1−h)·Σ_r π_{r|i,φ,e}·[r + V_e(m−1,w,i′,j′)]`; wicket cost `c(s) = [V_e(m,w,i,j) − V_e(m−1,w−1,ι(w),j) + δ_fresh]₊`; DAR = Σ (W_b − h̄)·c(s_b), a mean-zero martingale residual under the null. Mean wicket cost over 147,797 bowler-credited dismissals: 2.65 runs (median 2.56, 90th percentile 5.34); powerplay 3.10, death 2.52 — [arXiv 2608.18020 HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.18020v2)
- Impact: Ω(Bat) = Σ RAE_b − DAR^bat; Ω(Bowl) = −Σ RAE_b + DAR^bowl — [arXiv 2608.18020 HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.18020v2)
- Thresholds: Table 1 qualification counts batters n ≥ 30/100/500 and bowlers n ≥ 60/200; batter career leaderboard ≥ 500 balls; correlation analysis batters ≥ 500, bowlers ≥ 300; bowling career table 300 balls; batting season table 150 balls faced; bowling season table 120 balls bowled; tracked (mRAE) batters 400 tracked balls, bowlers 300 — [arXiv 2608.18020 HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.18020v2)
- Tuning/validation: the paper says there is "no tuning constant left to set" because κ_f is estimated by empirical Bayes. No cross-validation, held-out log-likelihood, split-half, year-to-year reliability or out-of-sample predictive validity is reported. Convergent validity (IPL, qualifying players): RAE/ball vs strike rate 0.82; Batting Impact vs RAE/ball 0.65; Batting Impact vs strike rate 0.54; Bowling Impact vs −RAE/ball 0.73; Bowling Impact vs economy −0.46; −RAE/ball vs economy −0.68; DAR/ball vs −RAE/ball 0.09; Bowling Impact vs DAR/ball 0.13 ("wicket-taking and run prevention are nearly orthogonal") — [arXiv 2608.18020 HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.18020v2)
- Recency/era: era handled as a year × phase multiplier (absorbs scoring inflation, Figure 3); no recency weighting or form window; ageing not discussed. Limitations named: context treated as exogenous to player identity (bowlers adapt line/length to batters); second-innings value function ignores live required rate; discrete quality tiers; cross-competition comparisons inherit a competition-strength discrepancy ("a standard-adjusted baseline is planned") — [arXiv 2608.18020 HTML (offset 100000)](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.18020v2)

### Cited Findings (Ganesh, "Simulation-Based Optimisation of Batting Order and Bowling Plans in T20 Cricket", arXiv 2604.13861v2 **[preprint]**)
- Data: 1,161 IPL matches 2008–2025 from Cricsheet via the yorkr R package; 273,735 delivery records, ≈264,800 legal balls used; wides and no-balls excluded — [arXiv 2604.13861 HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.13861v2)
- Phases by 0-indexed over: powerplay 0–5, middle 6–14, death 15–19 (Section 1 states overs 1–6, 7–15, 16–20 — internally inconsistent). Outcome set Ω = {W, 0, 1, 2, 3, 4, 6}; batter gets W when `wicketPlayerOut`; bowler credited unless run-out — [arXiv 2604.13861 HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.13861v2)
- Laplace smoothing α = 1: `c̃ = c + α`, `p̂ = c̃ / Σ c̃`; "maximum distortion is 7/n, negligible beyond about 100 deliveries per phase" — [arXiv 2604.13861 HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.13861v2)
- James–Stein-style blend: `p̄^φ = Σ_i c̃_i^φ / Σ_i Σ_o c̃_i^{o,φ}` (phase population average); `p_i^φ = λ_i^φ·p̂_i^φ + (1−λ_i^φ)·p̄^φ`; `λ_i^φ = n_i^φ / (n_i^φ + n_min)`, **n_min = 50**, justified only as "roughly 8 full overs of data"; n = 0 gives the population profile. Described as "a practical analogue" of James–Stein, not a derivation; the lower-MSE claim for n < n_min is asserted without measurement — [arXiv 2604.13861 HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.13861v2)
- No hard minimum sample, no recency weighting, no form window, no season/era adjustment; exponential decay weighting and in-match Bayesian updating are named as future extensions. No validation of n_min = 50 (no CV, sensitivity or out-of-sample test) — [arXiv 2604.13861 HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.13861v2)

### Cited Findings (other recent cricket implementations)
- Cricketing View "RAAR" (blog, 2025 IPL): expected runs per delivery from four inputs (balls faced so far by batter, balls remaining, wickets in hand, scoring rate at start of ball) via DNN regression; RAAR = actual − xR summed per innings; no opposition adjustment, no era normalisation, no minimum-ball threshold, no reliability statements — [Cricketing View substack **[blog]**](https://cricketingview.substack.com/p/runs-above-average-replacement-raar)
- Only other Dirichlet-on-cricket item found: a University of Manitoba thesis applying a smoothed Dirichlet prior to multinomial outcome data for T20I *bowlers*; baseball analogues are Null's nested Dirichlet over 14 batting outcomes and a 2024 Manitoba thesis comparing it with the plain Dirichlet-multinomial (search-snippet level only) — [Manitoba thesis](https://mspace.lib.umanitoba.ca/items/ec3e0511-47ce-432c-a3e3-ac57c5cde037); [Null, JQAS nested Dirichlet](https://www.degruyterbrill.com/document/doi/10.2202/1559-0410.1175/pdf)

### Inferences
- Three independent implementations land on a per-ball-rate prior of **40–60 pseudo-balls** (DPS c = 60 for the whole vector; Bandyopadhyay κ_μ = 60 for scoring rate, κ_η = 40 for hazard; Ganesh n_min = 50 per phase). That is the strongest convergence in the literature and a defensible default for shrinking a batter's run-rate or a bowler's economy toward a pool.
- For the **full 7-outcome Dirichlet vector**, the one data-estimated value is Bandyopadhyay's κ_π = 200 toward the (tier, phase, innings) mean. A larger pseudo-count for the vector than for the scalar rate makes sense: the rare cells (3s ≈ 0.6%, 2s, wickets ≈ 4–5%) need far more balls than the mean does to stabilise, and the Dirichlet pseudo-count governs all cells at once.
- Bandyopadhyay's κ_f formula is directly reusable to *estimate* pseudo-counts from your own data: κ = Var(r)/(μ₀²·τ²), with τ² the between-player variance of the per-ball-rate ratio after subtracting sampling variance (DerSimonian–Laird). This replaces hand-tuning. Note the κ for opposition *batter* (67) is much smaller than for opposition *bowler* (273): batters differ from each other far more (τ² = 0.0249 vs 0.0061), so batter effects need less shrinkage per ball than bowler effects — a bowler's identity should be shrunk roughly 4× harder than a batter's for equal balls.
- Ganesh's "distortion ≤ 7/n, negligible beyond ~100 balls per phase" and his n_min = 50 (half-weight) imply a display threshold around 100 balls per phase if phase splits are shown; nobody validates this.
- None of the three reports predictive validity by prior strength. The open question for a developer is still empirical: hold out a season and sweep κ.

### Gaps
- No preprint or paper found that sweeps prior strength against out-of-sample log-likelihood or next-season correlation for T20. The Bandyopadhyay κ values are "optimal" only in the empirical-Bayes sense (variance-component estimation), not in a predictive-validation sense.
- No 2024–2026 arXiv paper found on T20 ratings with recency weighting; the Glicko-for-Test paper (arXiv 2603.02574) is a different format.
- No source labelled "Ganesh 2026 phase blend" by that name; the n/(n+50) per-phase blend is in arXiv 2604.13861 (Tinniam V Ganesh, April 2026), which is a lineup-optimisation paper rather than a ratings paper.

## Key Question 3 — Reliability / stabilisation points for T20 metrics; baseball stabilisation methodology and whether it has been applied to cricket

### Takeaway
No published cricket study measures split-half reliability or a stabilisation point for T20 strike rate, boundary %, dot %, dismissal rate, economy or bowling strike rate; the only cricket "stability" numbers are DPS's career-vs-form correlations (0.77 batting, 0.91 bowling) and Bandyopadhyay's convergent correlations. The baseball methodology (Carleton split-half at r = 0.70; FanGraphs Cronbach's-alpha curves; Tango/Medvedovsky "padding" chosen by RMSE against rest-of-season) is well documented and transfers directly to balls faced / bowled as the denominator.

### Cited Findings (baseball methodology)
- Carleton's criterion: a stat is "stable" when the correlation between two same-size samples of the same players reaches 0.7 ("i.e. R² of .49"); Dolinar & Pemstein's later work uses the same .49 threshold and is "technically Cronbach's Alpha". Hitter stabilisation points: K% 60 PA; BB% 120 PA; HBP% 240 PA; 1B% 290 PA; XBH% 1,610 PA; HR% 170 PA; AVG 910 AB; OBP 460 PA; SLG 320 AB; ISO 160 AB; GB% 80 BIP; FB% 80 BIP; LD% 600 BIP; HR/FB 50 FB; BABIP 820 BIP. Pitchers: K% 70 BF; BB% 170 BF; HBP% 640 BF; 1B% 670 BF; XBH% 1,450 BF; HR% 1,320 BF; AVG 630 BF; OBP 540 BF; SLG 550 AB; ISO 630 AB; GB% 70 BIP; FB% 70 BIP; LD% 650 BIP; HR/FB 400 FB; BABIP 2,000 BIP. "A statistic doesn't stabilize, it becomes more stable." — [FanGraphs Library, Sample Size **[blog]**](https://library.fangraphs.com/principles/sample-size/)
- Method detail: Carleton found the PA count where alpha hit .70 "and then halved the PA/AB/BIP value" (because the split-half compares two half-samples). Dolinar & Pemstein instead computed Cronbach's alpha on Retrosheet 2009–2014 with random samples of 10–600 PA (capped at 600 PA / 500 AB / 400 BIP because beyond that "results become erratic"), removing IBB, bunts and non-batting events; conclusion "there is no single point at which a stat becomes stable"; alpha is "the proportion of true-talent variance to the observed variance" and depends on the spread of talent in the sample ("Restricting to high-PA players will decrease the standard deviation", lowering reliability). Regression: "If a stat has a low reliability, the sample's average is a better estimation of the true talent." Carleton (in comments) says he used "Cronbach/KR-21" and that halved vs non-halved should be "tested for predictive performance" — [FanGraphs, "A New Way to Look at Sample Size" **[blog]**](https://blogs.fangraphs.com/a-new-way-to-look-at-sample-size)
- Older (2007-era) Carleton thresholds quoted by Grantland: 50 PA Swing%; 100 PA contact rate; 150 PA K rate, LD rate, pitches/PA; 200 PA BB rate, GB rate; 250 PA FB rate; 300 PA HR rate, HR/FB; 500 PA OBP, SLG, OPS, 1B rate, pop-up rate; 550 PA ISO; BABIP "doesn't stabilize even after 650 plate appearances". Pitchers: 150 BF K/PA, GB, LD; 200 BF FB rate; 500 BF K/BB, pop-up; 550 BF BB/PA — [Grantland **[blog]**](https://grantland.com/the-triangle/fantasy-fiesta-when-can-we-trust-the-numbers/)
- Predictive alternative ("padding", credited to Medvedovsky and Tango): regressed rate = (S + X·league rate)/(PA + X); choose X by computing each player's running K% after every game, forming the padded estimate, and using R's `optimize` to pick the X minimising RMSE between the padded rate and rest-of-year performance. "Low X means the stat stabilizes quickly, and high X means it takes more PA." — [Down on the Farm substack **[blog, paywalled]**](https://downonthefarm.substack.com/p/estimating-minor-league-stabilization)
- Variance decomposition underpinning all of this: var(observed) = var(talent) + var(luck), with binomial luck variance shrinking with trials — [Gelman, Statistical Modeling **[blog]**](https://statmodeling.stat.columbia.edu/2014/06/27/quantifying-luck-vs-skill-sports/); Tango's Gaussian prior/posterior worked example — [Tangotiger archives **[blog]**](https://tangotiger.net/archives/stud0322.shtml)
- Critique: fantasy writers note the "stabilization point" is usually defined at 50% skill/50% luck and that stats "don't stabilize" in any step-function sense; Tango's critique of Carleton: PA should not be the denominator for every stat (BABIP depends on balls in play) — [RotoWire **[blog]**](https://www.rotowire.com/baseball/article/the-z-files-the-fallacy-of-stabilization-and-an-early-look-at-home-runs-56442)
- Split-half methodology from psychometrics: a 2021 review of four splitting schemes (first–second half, odd–even, permuted, Monte Carlo) recommends Monte Carlo splitting as "the most robust method" against confounds such as drift and autocorrelation — [Pronk et al., Psychonomic Bulletin & Review 2021 **[PR]**](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.3758/s13423-021-01948-3.pdf)

### Cited Findings (cricket-side)
- DPS career vs current-form E(D) correlation: 0.77 pure batsmen, 0.91 bowlers, 0.68 all-rounders (players with ≥ 250 balls) — [moneyball.pdf **[PR]**](https://www.sfu.ca/~tswartz/papers/moneyball.pdf)
- Selection-bias warning relevant to any minimum-ball threshold: "There are no pure batsmen who are much worse than E(D) = 0, likely because their poor performances prevented them from playing long enough to face 250 balls." — [moneyball.pdf **[PR]**](https://www.sfu.ca/~tswartz/papers/moneyball.pdf)
- Within-innings (not career) stabilisation: ESPNcricinfo found a batter's strike rate reaches its "cruising value" at about 15–20 balls faced in the middle overs and fluctuates without trend thereafter (search snippet; page 403) — [ESPNcricinfo **[blog/media]**](https://www.espncricinfo.com/story/do-teams-benefit-when-their-batters-play-long-innings-in-t20s-1324594); Misra (2026, 281,256 IPL deliveries) finds strike rate rises from 103 on a batter's first five legal balls to 150 on balls 30–34 and argues this is not mainly survivorship bias — [Misra, "set batter" 2026 **[preprint/working paper]**](https://www.cs.columbia.edu/~misra/set-batter-2026.pdf)

### Inferences
- Standard result linking the two framings (not stated verbatim in any fetched source, derived from the variance decomposition above): reliability at n trials is r(n) = var_talent/(var_talent + var_luck/n) = n/(n + k) where k = var_luck/var_talent is exactly the pseudo-count in `(S + k·league)/(n + k)`. Hence Carleton's r = 0.70 sample size is n₀.₇ = (0.7/0.3)·k ≈ 2.33k, and the pseudo-count is k ≈ 0.43 × the r = 0.70 stabilisation point; the r = 0.5 point equals k itself. Bandyopadhyay's κ_f = Var(r)/(μ₀²τ²) is this same k written in rate-ratio units.
- Translating the convergent 40–60 pseudo-ball prior back through this identity: r = 0.5 at ≈ 50 balls, r = 0.7 at ≈ 115–140 balls, r = 0.8 at ≈ 200–240 balls. That is consistent with Bandyopadhyay's 150-ball season threshold and DPS's 250-ball career threshold sitting at the r ≈ 0.7–0.8 band.
- Baseball analogues suggest the ordering of T20 metric reliabilities: event rates that happen often per ball (dot %, non-boundary scoring %) should stabilise fastest (like K%, GB%); boundary % (≈ 15–18% of balls) in the middle; dismissal rate (≈ 4–5% of balls) and bowling strike rate slowest (like HR% / BABIP). Economy (runs per ball, a mean) should be more reliable than bowling average (ratio of two noisy counts), matching DPS's recommendation that strike rate and economy be "slightly preferred" to average in T20.
- A developer can measure all of this directly from Cricsheet with Monte Carlo split-half: for each player with ≥ 2n balls in a window, randomly assign balls to two halves, compute the metric in each, correlate across players, repeat 200+ times, and read off the n at which median r hits 0.5 (= k) or 0.7. Report alongside next-season correlation (restricted to qualified players) because split-half within a season ignores genuine skill drift and ageing.

### Gaps
- No cricket source (peer-reviewed or blog) found that reports split-half reliability, Cronbach's alpha, ICC or a stabilisation point for any T20 metric. The CricViz / Himanish Ganjoo / Amol Desai methodology posts could not be located via search (Ganjoo's "True Strike Rate" is described only second-hand as runs above par per situation).
- Nothing found on how the stabilisation points differ by phase (powerplay vs death), which matters because death-overs batting has a higher variance per ball.

## Key Question 4 — How much to adjust a batter's counts for opposition bowling quality and match situation (over, wickets) when aggregating across matches

### Takeaway
Both the peer-reviewed simulator and the 2026 preprint treat situation (over × wickets) as a shared multiplicative factor on the outcome vector and opposition as an explicit per-identity effect; Bandyopadhyay's deviance table shows opposition bowler, venue and wicket state are the largest sources of expected-run variation, with opposition-bowler effects needing κ ≈ 273 balls of shrinkage and a between-bowler sd of the run multiplier of about √0.0061 ≈ 8%.

### Cited Findings
- DPS situation adjustment: `p_iowj = τ_owj p_i70j / Σ_k τ_owk p_i70k`, so all balls are "transformed to the case o = 7 and w = 0" before pooling a batter's counts; the paper explicitly expects τ_ow4 > 1 and τ_ow6 > 1 when aggression rises — [t20sim.pdf **[PR]**](https://www.sfu.ca/~tswartz/papers/t20sim.pdf)
- DPS opposition adjustment: batsman-vs-bowler outcome vector `p_{i1,owj} + q_{i2,owj} − p̄_owj` (eq. 7), where the bowler's q vector is estimated with the same empirical-Bayes shrinkage — [t20sim.pdf **[PR]**](https://www.sfu.ca/~tswartz/papers/t20sim.pdf)
- Bandyopadhyay factor sizes (Table 3): between-cell variance of the run multiplier τ² = 0.0034 for wickets-fallen × phase, 0.0093 for phase × innings, 0.0054 for venue (ground × 3-year bucket), 0.0061 for opposition bowler identity, 0.0249 for opposition batter identity; corresponding EB pseudo-counts 485, 180, 311, 273, 67. "Opposition bowler, venue and wicket contribute the most deviance." All factors p < 10⁻³⁰⁰ — [arXiv 2608.18020 HTML **[preprint]**](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.18020v2)
- Bandyopadhyay fits opposition one role at a time (opposing bowler identity when evaluating batters; opposing batter identity when evaluating bowlers), and flags that context is treated as exogenous: "a batter who forces uncomfortable areas may have a slightly deflated expected value" because bowlers adapt line/length — [arXiv 2608.18020 HTML **[preprint]**](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.18020v2)
- Wicket cost by phase (the "situation" value of a dismissal): mean 2.65 runs overall, 3.10 in the powerplay, 2.52 at the death — [arXiv 2608.18020 HTML **[preprint]**](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.18020v2)
- Cricketing View RAAR conditions expected runs on (balls faced so far, balls remaining, wickets in hand, current run rate) but not on opposition — [Cricketing View **[blog]**](https://cricketingview.substack.com/p/runs-above-average-replacement-raar)
- Test-cricket precedent for opposition-strength adjusted averages (not T20): ESPNcricinfo "It Figures" and Cricketweb pieces adjust averages for "relative strength of bat and ball" and "rating of opposition strength" (search snippets only) — [ESPNcricinfo It Figures](https://www.espncricinfo.com/story/adjusting-averages-to-account-for-bowling-strengths-612664); [Cricketweb adjusted averages](https://www.cricketweb.net/adjusted-averages-or-what-do-vinod-kambli-and-mick-hucknall-out-of-simply-red-have-in-common/)

### Inferences
- Order of magnitude: a τ² of 0.0061 for opposition bowler means the typical bowler shifts a batter's expected runs per ball by roughly ±8% (one sd of the multiplier); the wicket-state factor (τ² = 0.0034, ≈ ±6%) and scenario factor (τ² = 0.0093, ≈ ±10%) are similar in size. For a card-game rating aggregated over a tournament, the situation adjustment matters most for players whose balls are concentrated in one phase (openers vs finishers), and the opposition adjustment matters most in tournaments with uneven bowling attacks (associates vs full members).
- The cheapest robust implementation is DPS-style: estimate the shared τ_owj (or phase × wickets) multipliers once from all data, divide each ball's outcome counts by them to put every ball on the baseline state, then shrink. Bandyopadhyay's multiplicative backfitting is the same idea extended to venue, era and opponent.
- Because opposition-batter identity has κ = 67 while opposition-bowler has κ = 273, when adjusting *bowlers* for the batters they faced you can trust ~70 balls of batter history, but when adjusting *batters* for bowlers faced you need ~270 balls of bowler history before the bowler's effect is half-weighted.

### Gaps
- No source quantifies how much a batter's *rating ranking* changes with vs without opposition adjustment in T20 (only deviance contributions).
- No published value for how to shrink a batter's phase-specific vector toward his own overall vector (hierarchical player → player-phase); Bandyopadhyay shrinks (tier, phase, innings) cells toward the phase pool, not toward the individual.

## Key Question 5 — How far back T20 data stays informative given scoring inflation; per-season vs rolling-era normalisation

### Takeaway
The only quantitative era treatment with a reported strength is Bandyopadhyay's year × phase multiplier (69 cells, τ² = 0.0016, κ = 1,043): era effects are real but small relative to player and opposition effects, which argues for a smooth rolling normalisation (year × phase or 3-year buckets) rather than hard per-season rescaling. League-level inflation evidence is media/blog-grade.

### Cited Findings
- Bandyopadhyay: era is a year × phase multiplier γ^era (69 cells) that "absorbs scoring inflation (Figure 3)"; τ² = 0.0016 (sd ≈ 4% of expected runs per ball), κ = 1,043; venue effects are bucketed as ground × 3-year window (1,268 cells, τ² = 0.0054), i.e. the venue dimension is itself a rolling 3-year normalisation. Grand mean μ₀ = 1.2425 runs per legal ball over 2005–2026 — [arXiv 2608.18020 HTML **[preprint]**](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.18020v2)
- CricViz/Wisden (IPL 2018, mid-season): average run rate 8.22 rpo "the highest ever"; powerplay 8.13 rpo, middle 7.66 rpo (both fastest in IPL history); death 9.18 rpo (fourth fastest); 18% of deliveries hit for 4 or 6; attacking-shot share "broadly stable", so "the scoring rate has gone up because of an increase in the regularity of boundaries" not intent; wickets lost more regularly than any previous season — [Wisden / CricViz **[blog/media]**](https://www.wisden.com/cricket-features/ipl-run-scoring-t20-trend-cricviz)
- ESPNcricinfo (search snippet; page 403): top-eight batters scored at 143 runs per 100 balls in the four years after Covid waned vs 132 in the four years before — [ESPNcricinfo stats **[media]**](https://www.espncricinfo.com/page2/content/story/1522257.html)
- Normalisation factor used in a cross-era cricket paper (ODI, not T20): scale each year's batting runs by (all-years average team score)/(that year's average), a method "originally introduced to correctly assess the impact of scientific papers published in different years" (search snippet; PDF not readable) — [Sadekar et al., arXiv 2401.15161 **[preprint]**](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2401.15161)
- Test-cricket era-adjustment argument that both the level and the *spread* of averages must be adjusted when comparing eras (search snippet) — [Cricketweb, Kettle **[blog]**](https://www.cricketweb.net/?p=23923)
- DPS pooled nine years (Jan 2005–Aug 2014) with no era adjustment at all — [moneyball.pdf **[PR]**](https://www.sfu.ca/~tswartz/papers/moneyball.pdf); Ganesh pooled IPL 2008–2025 with no era adjustment — [arXiv 2604.13861 **[preprint]**](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.13861v2)

### Inferences
- An ~8% rise in top-order strike rate across a four-year boundary (132 → 143) is about twice the era-cell sd Bandyopadhyay estimates (≈ 4%) because his cells are year × phase and shrunk hard (κ ≈ 1,000 balls); the two are consistent with inflation being gradual and phase-dependent (death overs inflate more than middle). A per-year, per-phase multiplier estimated from all players (tens of thousands of balls per cell, so shrinkage is irrelevant in practice) is the right granularity; per-season rescaling of each *player* separately would be far noisier.
- Because era effects (±4%) are an order of magnitude smaller than the sampling noise of a 100-ball player sample, the practical constraint on "how far back" is form drift and ageing, not inflation. Normalise for era anyway (it is nearly free), but set the window from the recency/ageing evidence.
- The 3-year venue bucket in Bandyopadhyay is a useful template for a rolling-era normalisation: define "era" as a 3-year rolling window rather than a calendar season so that a tournament-time rating does not jump at 1 January.

### Gaps
- No study found that tests, for T20, whether player ratings built on 12 vs 24 vs 36 months of data predict the next tournament better. Nothing found on per-season vs rolling normalisation head-to-head.
- The ESPNcricinfo inflation numbers could only be verified from a search snippet.

## Key Question 6 — T20-specific peak-age estimates that would justify age-based windows

### Takeaway
No peer-reviewed T20 peak-age estimate was found. Peer-reviewed ODI evidence (1971–2000 data) puts batter peak at ≈ 27–30 and bowler peak at ≈ 22–26; a practitioner (Dan Weston) uses a 28–31 rule of thumb across formats; ICC Test rating peaks are ≈ 29.7 years for both roles. Age is therefore a weak justification for a rating *window* but a reasonable input for *projecting* a rating forward.

### Cited Findings
- Frontiers in Behavioral Economics (2024), men's ODI 1971–2000, 22,114 observations (14,112 batter, 8,002 bowler) from 210 players (> 25 matches, ≥ 5-year career, ≤ 1 break of a year+): inverted-U in age; "peak-performance is attained for bowlers at around 22–26 years and for batters at about 27–30 years"; strike rate peaks around 30; economy rate improves with age. Method: OLS and player fixed-effects with age and age² (batters, runs: FE col. 2 coefficients 5.8535 and −0.1000; with full controls 0.9919* and −0.0184**; bowlers, wickets: FE 0.0915 and −0.0018, full-control age term not significant). The paper chose ODI because T20Is "are about two decades old" and does not analyse T20 — [Frontiers **[PR]**](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/behavioral-economics/articles/10.3389/frbhe.2024.1492735/full)
- Dan Weston (Kent CCC senior data analyst, 2022): "the average player peaks between the ages of 28-31 … it's slightly different for various formats and player roles, but it's a good enough rule of thumb"; applied to England's 2022 T20 World Cup squad (9 of 13 past peak). No data or method shown; post partly paywalled — [Dan Weston substack **[blog]**](https://danweston.substack.com/p/whats-gone-wrong-with-england)
- ICC Test-ranking peaks (search snippet, icc-cricket.com): batters' peak ratings at about 29 years 9 months, bowlers 29 years 8 months; pace ≈ 29y2m, spin ≈ 30y6m — [ICC **[media]**](https://www.icc-cricket.com/news/reliance-mobile-icc-player-rankings)
- Boundary Line (Medium, 403 — search snippets only): ODI batting peaks are "flatter and last longer" than Test peaks; "the level of competition is lower in ODI cricket, hence the later peak and slower decline"; boundary share "has held up better with age than non-boundary scoring"; the author reuses "an older personal T20 model" rather than publishing T20 results — [Boundary Line white-ball age **[blog]**](https://medium.com/boundary-line/the-role-of-age-in-white-ball-batting-performance-5ee9e272646f); [Boundary Line red-ball age **[blog]**](https://medium.com/boundary-line/the-role-of-age-in-red-ball-batting-performance-377d7d89afe4)
- A Thai statistics-journal paper studies age vs composite IPL performance across seasons; findings not readable in the retrieved text — [Thai J. Statistics **[PR, unverified content]**](https://ph02.tci-thaijo.org/index.php/thaistat/article/download/37774/31385)

### Inferences
- With peak windows this wide (27–31) and ODI-derived, age cannot justify a fixed-length window on its own; what it does justify is an age-dependent *prior mean* when projecting a rating to a future tournament (shrink a 34-year-old's recent form toward a lower expected level than a 27-year-old's). The Boundary Line snippet (boundary share more age-robust than strike rotation) suggests, if implemented per outcome category, that dot/single rates should decay with age before 4/6 rates do — but that is blog-grade and unverified.
- The DPS recency decay (r = 0.88, ~8 effective matches) already dominates any age effect for in-tournament ratings; age matters when the window is 24–36 months.

### Gaps
- No T20-specific peak age (batting or bowling, by role) found in any peer-reviewed or methodology-disclosing source. The Weston age-curve work and the Boundary Line T20 model are not published in enough detail to implement.

## Developer decision summary (synthesis of the above; every number traces to a cited finding, the recommendations themselves are inferences)

### (a) Form window and recency decay
- Published parameter: DPS r = 0.88 per match (half-life ≈ 5.4 matches, effective ≈ 8 matches), chosen by posterior maximisation on 282 matches; DPS report career-vs-form correlations of 0.77 (bat) / 0.91 (bowl), so form and career disagree more for batters than bowlers. Bandyopadhyay and Ganesh use no decay at all with 18–21 years of data — [t20sim.pdf](https://www.sfu.ca/~tswartz/papers/t20sim.pdf); [moneyball.pdf](https://www.sfu.ca/~tswartz/papers/moneyball.pdf); [arXiv 2608.18020](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.18020v2); [arXiv 2604.13861](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.13861v2)
- Inference: for a tournament rating, 0.88/match is a *form* setting; a *skill* setting should be slower. A per-month decay with half-life 12–18 months (≈ 0.94–0.96 per month) keeps 24–36 months of data materially weighted while still halving two-year-old balls, and keeps the effective sample above the 40–60-ball prior for most squad players. Validate by sweeping the decay against next-tournament RAE/ball correlation (the "padding" RMSE procedure generalises to decay).

### (b) Pseudo-count / prior strength
- Per-ball scalar rates: c = 60 (DPS, insensitive on 50–80), κ_μ = 60 scoring rate and κ_η = 40 hazard (Bandyopadhyay, EB-estimated), n_min = 50 per phase (Ganesh, hand-set). Full 7-category Dirichlet vector toward phase mean: κ_π = 200 (Bandyopadhyay). Context factors (if you adjust for them): κ ≈ 180 scenario, 485 wickets, 311 venue, 273 opposition bowler, 67 opposition batter, 1,043 era, 1,249 bowler type.
- Inference: use Dirichlet(κ·p̄_phase) with κ ≈ 150–200 on the 7-vector per phase, which gives the implied scalar run-rate about 50 pseudo-balls of weight once the vector is collapsed; or estimate κ from your own data with κ = Var(r)/(μ₀²·τ²) (DerSimonian–Laird τ²). Shrink bowlers' identity effects harder than batters' (κ 273 vs 67).

### (c) Minimum balls before display
- Published: DPS ≥ 250 balls faced / 250 bowled (career); Bandyopadhyay 500 (career bat), 300 (career bowl), 150 (season bat), 120 (season bowl), with Table 1 tiers at 30/100/500 bat and 60/200 bowl; Ganesh none (continuous shrinkage, half-weight at 50, "negligible" Laplace distortion beyond ~100 per phase).
- Inference via r = n/(n+k): with k ≈ 50 pseudo-balls, n = 50 gives r ≈ 0.5, n ≈ 120–150 gives r ≈ 0.7, n ≈ 250 gives r ≈ 0.83. A sensible rule is: display a shrunken rating at any n (it is always the posterior mean), show a confidence band or "provisional" tag below ~120–150 balls (r < 0.7), and require ≈ 250 for unflagged career ratings. Beware the DPS selection effect: thresholds truncate the bad tail.

### (d) Measuring year-to-year reliability
- Within-window: Monte Carlo split-half (random ball assignment, many repetitions) → Cronbach's alpha / correlation vs half-sample size; read k from r = 0.5 and the "stable" point from r = 0.7; halve or not per Carleton's caution, and test predictive performance either way — [FanGraphs](https://blogs.fangraphs.com/a-new-way-to-look-at-sample-size); [Pronk et al. 2021](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.3758/s13423-021-01948-3.pdf)
- Across seasons: Pearson/Spearman of season N vs N+1 for qualified players (note alpha depends on talent spread of the qualified pool), plus predictive RMSE of the padded/decayed estimate vs rest-of-season or next-tournament outcomes, choosing k and decay by `optimize` on RMSE — [Down on the Farm](https://downonthefarm.substack.com/p/estimating-minor-league-stabilization)
- Report convergent correlations against simple metrics as Bandyopadhyay does (RAE/ball vs SR 0.82; −RAE/ball vs economy −0.68) so a reader can see what the shrunken rating adds — [arXiv 2608.18020](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.18020v2)
