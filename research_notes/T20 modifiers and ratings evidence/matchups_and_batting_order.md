## KQ1. Handedness × bowling-type matchup effects (spin type vs batter hand; left-arm pace vs RHB)

### Takeaway
The only peer-reviewed T20 estimate is Kerruish, McRobert & Jamil (2025): on 23,084 elite-spinner deliveries across six T20 competitions, spin turning *into* the left-hander (leg-spin, SLA) carries ~1.3–1.4× the per-ball odds of conceding runs relative to off-spin (turning away) against left-handers, while against right-handers off-spin and SLA (turning in) take wickets ~20% less often than leg-spin; googlies to RHB raise wicket odds 1.72×. These are raw per-ball logistic regressions with no control for bowler or batter quality, so treat them as upper bounds on the pure matchup effect. No peer-reviewed estimate exists for left-arm pace vs RHB; CricViz publishes no matchup methodology.

### Cited Findings
- **Kerruish, McRobert & Jamil (2025) [peer-reviewed]**, *European Journal of Sport Science* 25(5):e12296, DOI 10.1002/ejsc.12296. Data: Opta ball-by-ball for ICC WT20 2016, BPL 2019/20, CPL 2020, IPL 2020, BBL 2019/20, PSL 2020; 23,084 spin deliveries after dropping variations <1% of balls (from 23,521): 15,056 to RHB, 8,028 to LHB. Method: eight independent binary logistic regressions (outcome = runs conceded yes/no, wicket yes/no; predictor = bowler type or bowling variation; balls treated as independent; software JASP 0.14). No controls for bowler/batter quality or phase. — [PMC11985319](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11985319)
- Kerruish et al. primary analysis, **LHB, reference = right-arm off-spin**: runs OR — right-arm leg-spin 1.298 (95% CI 1.171–1.438, p<0.001); left-arm orthodox 1.424 (1.256–1.613, p<0.001); left-arm unorthodox 1.292 (0.965–1.732, p=0.085). Wicket OR — leg-spin 1.147 (0.908–1.449, p=0.252); SLA 1.054 (0.791–1.404, p=0.719); left-arm unorthodox 1.326 (0.724–2.430, p=0.360). I.e. against left-handers, off-spin (turning away from LHB) concedes runs significantly less often than leg-spin or SLA (turning in), with no significant wicket difference. — [PMC11985319](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11985319)
- Kerruish et al. primary analysis, **RHB, reference = right-arm leg-spin**: runs OR — off-spin 0.994 (0.915–1.079, p=0.883); SLA 0.918 (0.849–0.994, p=0.035); left-arm unorthodox 1.207 (0.995–1.464, p=0.056). Wicket OR — off-spin 0.803 (0.662–0.973, p=0.025); SLA 0.792 (0.658–0.953, p=0.014); left-arm unorthodox 0.913 (CI as printed 0.598–0.834 is inconsistent with the OR; reporting error). I.e. against right-handers, leg-spin (turning away) takes wickets ~25% more often than off-spin or SLA (turning in); SLA is ~8% less likely to concede runs than leg-spin. — [PMC11985319](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11985319)
- Kerruish et al. secondary analysis by **variation, RHB (reference = off-break)**: googly runs OR 0.825 (0.740–0.919, p<0.001), wicket OR 1.722 (1.377–2.153, p<0.001); carrom ball runs OR 0.732 (0.536–1.000, p=0.050; text says p<0.001 — discrepancy), wicket OR 2.037 (1.141–3.633, p=0.016); leg-spinner (stock ball) runs OR 1.155 (1.070–1.245, p<0.001), wicket OR 1.092 (0.914–1.305, p=0.333). **LHB (reference = off-break)**: leg-spinner runs OR 1.408 (1.251–1.584, p<0.001); arm ball runs OR 1.268 (1.047–1.536, p=0.015); googly runs OR 0.970 (p=0.642), wicket OR 1.300 (0.984–1.718, p=0.065). Authors' conclusion: match-ups where the ball spins away from the batter are "particularly effective at limiting runs"; right-arm leg-spin is significantly more likely to dismiss RHB; googly/carrom to RHB restrict runs and take wickets. — [PMC11985319](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11985319); open-access PDF also at [LJMU repository](https://researchonline.ljmu.ac.uk/id/eprint/27042/)
- **Ganjoo, ESPNcricinfo "Do match-ups work in T20? The data says yes" (17 Apr 2021) [journalism]**: IPL baseline run rate and dismissal numbers from the previous three IPL seasons split by batter hand × bowling style × phase; snippet only (page 403): "for left-hand batters, their overall dismissal rate facing offbreak bowlers is 4.3 compared to just 3.6 versus slow left-arm" (units not visible in snippet; context implies dismissals per 100 balls); also reports that in the powerplay right-handers score slower and are dismissed more often than left-handers, and that teams can "hide" left-arm spinners from unfavourable match-ups (selection bias caveat). Defines a Match-up Rating Index (MRI): bowler's runs-per-ball divided by the average for bowlers of his type in that match-up and phase; MRI=1 is average, <1 economical. — search snippets of [ESPNcricinfo story 1258833](https://www.espncricinfo.com/ci/content/story/1258833.html) (403 on direct fetch); companion Substack post by the same author confirms the piece and links spreadsheets by batting hand × bowling type × phase but gives no numbers — [Deep Point](https://www.deeppoint-hg.com/p/an-article-on-matchups-in-t20-cricket)
- **Kimber, ESPNcricinfo/ESPN (2 Jul 2019), ODI data, stats by Shiva Jayaraman [journalism]**: left-handers vs spin (Champions Trophy 2017 → start of WC 2019) score 0.7 runs per over slower against off-spin than leg-spin but average 7.25 fewer runs per dismissal against leg-spin; i.e. off-spin (away from LHB) restricts, leg-spin (into LHB) takes wickets. ODI, not T20. — [ESPN](https://www.espn.co.uk/cricket/story/_/id/27102343/do-right-left-pairs-crease-work-complicated)
- **Bandyopadhyay (2026) [preprint, arXiv 2608.18020]** models bowler sub-type × phase but explicitly does *not* include batter handedness; no handedness matchup estimates. — [arXiv HTML v2](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.18020v2)
- **Davis, Perera & Swartz (2015) [peer-reviewed]**, *ANZJS* 57(1):55–71: individual batter-vs-bowler matchup model: for batter i1 facing bowler i2 in over o with w wickets down, outcome-j probability = p_{i1,o,w,j} + q_{i2,o,w,j} − p̄_{o,w,j}, where p = batter's multinomial characteristic vs average bowler, q = bowler's characteristic vs average batter (same empirical-Bayes estimation), p̄ = league average for that state; reduces to q when batter is average and to p when bowler is average. Data: 250 full-member T20Is 2005–2013, 30,458 batting outcomes, 384 batters. No handedness or bowling-type covariate. — [SFU PDF](https://www.sfu.ca/~tswartz/papers/t20sim.pdf)
- **Dan Weston "Quantifying Match-Ups" (9 Mar 2023) [blog, paywalled]**: only the framing is visible — individual head-to-head records lack "context and sample size" so conclusions "can't really be drawn". No numbers accessible. — [Substack](https://danweston.substack.com/p/quantifying-match-ups)
- **CricViz**: public descriptions define xR as "expected run total for a batter given the deliveries faced" based on ball-tracking (line/length/speed of similar deliveries) and xW vs "a typical batter"; no published matchup methodology located. — [App Store listing](https://apps.apple.com/app/id1044644979); a third-party blogger notes CricViz's expected definitions are opaque — [bfern blog](https://bfern.github.io/posts/2023-05-17-introducing-contribution-score)

### Inferences
- Direction-of-turn is the operative variable, not spin "type" per se: convert labels to {turns away, turns in, wrist-spin variation} relative to the striker's hand. Reimplementation needs: batter hand, bowler arm (L/R), bowler type (pace / off-spin / leg-spin / SLA / left-arm wrist), and ideally variation flags (googly, carrom, arm ball).
- Order-of-magnitude for a game engine, from Kerruish ORs: ball-turning-away ≈ −8% to −30% odds of conceding runs on a ball vs turning-in (depends on reference), and leg-spin-to-RHB ≈ +25% dismissal odds vs finger spin to RHB. Because quality is uncontrolled and some leagues over-represent certain bowlers, halve these for a conservative "pure matchup" multiplier.
- Googly/carrom to RHB (wicket OR 1.7–2.0) is the largest single effect in the literature and would justify a "mystery/wrist-spin variation" trait.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed T20 estimate of left-arm pace vs RHB (angle effect); only per-player fantasy-site splits exist (unreliable). Needs hand-labelled bowler arm.
- No study controlling for bowler and batter quality while estimating handedness × type effects in T20; Kerruish et al. is raw. Bandyopadhyay's framework (bowler-type × phase multipliers with opposition shrinkage) could be extended with a handedness factor but the paper didn't.
- ESPNcricinfo's full matchup tables (Ganjoo 2021, "Smart Stats" MRI) inaccessible (403 on story and print URLs). Numbers above are snippet-only.
- CricViz matchup methodology: not published.

## KQ2. Bowler type by phase (spin vs pace effectiveness by over) and batter skill vs pace/spin by phase

### Takeaway
Bandyopadhyay (2026) gives the only published bowler-type × phase multiplier table for T20 (2.73M legal balls, 19 competitions): leg-spin runs 1.056× expected in the powerplay but 0.950× at the death; left-arm pace is cheapest in the powerplay (0.951) and right-arm pace dearest at the death (1.033); finger spin is cheapest at the death (0.95–0.96). Phase itself is a far larger effect (death ≈ +13–19% runs/ball; dismissal hazard roughly doubles at the death). Batter-level pace/spin skill by phase is handled in the literature by per-player phase profiles with shrinkage (Ganesh 2026) rather than published population estimates; Misra (2026) shows the "getting set" SR gain is larger vs spin (+41.5 SR at balls 30–34) than vs pace (+34.8).

### Cited Findings
- **Bandyopadhyay, "Context-adjusted Player Evaluation for Twenty20 Cricket" (arXiv 2608.18020, v2 27 Aug 2026) [preprint]**. Data: 2,819,700 deliveries (2,727,420 legal), 12,492 matches, 19 competitions, Feb 2005–May/Jun 2026; IPL subset 295,629 deliveries / 1,243 matches. Model (Eq. 1): expected runs per legal ball μ(x) = μ0 · γ^scen(phase, innings) · γ^era(year, phase) · γ^wkt(phase, wickets fallen) · γ^type(phase, bowler sub-type) · γ^ven(ground, 3-yr bucket) · γ^opp; μ0 = 1.2425 runs/ball, Var(r)=2.578; fitted by iterated backfitting with empirical-Bayes shrinkage per factor (pseudo-count κ: scenario 180, era 1,043, wicket 485, bowler-type 1,249, venue 311, opposition bowler 273, opposition batter 67). — [arXiv HTML v2](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.18020v2)
- Bandyopadhyay **Table S2, bowler sub-type × phase multiplier γ^type (runs per ball relative to expectation)**: Right-arm pace PP 1.016 / Mid 1.025 / Death 1.033; Left-arm pace 0.951 / 1.026 / 1.007; Leg-spin 1.056 / 0.998 / 0.950; Off-spin 1.005 / 0.988 / 0.961; Left-arm spin 0.988 / 0.951 / 0.953; Other 0.990 / 1.001 / 1.009; Unknown 0.958 / 0.985 / 1.011. Phase-averaged: RA pace 1.028, LA pace 0.998, leg-spin 1.005, off-spin 0.989, SLA 0.968 (21 cells, range 0.97–1.03, τ²=0.0013). — [arXiv HTML v2 (supplement section)](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.18020v2)
- Bandyopadhyay **Table 4 scenario multipliers (phase × innings)**: PP 0.959 (inn 1) / 0.999 (inn 2); Middle 0.968 / 0.975; Death 1.188 / 1.132 — i.e. death overs +18.8% (inn 1) and +13.2% (inn 2) runs/ball vs base; powerplay 0–4% below base. — [arXiv HTML v2](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.18020v2)
- Bandyopadhyay **Table 5 wicket multipliers γ^wkt (phase × wickets fallen 0…10)**: PP 1.047, 1.025, 0.920, 0.825, 0.746, 0.716, 0.851, 0.911, 1.008, 0.984; Middle 1.136, 1.081, 1.036, 0.988, 0.938, 0.889, 0.840, 0.770, 0.735, 0.657, 0.977; Death 0.986, 1.164, 1.150, 1.117, 1.083, 1.028, 0.975, 0.895, 0.824, 0.692, 0.751. — [arXiv HTML v2](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.18020v2)
- Bandyopadhyay **league baseline dismissal hazard per legal ball (Table S9)**: pooled inn 1 PP 0.0448, Mid 0.0471, Death 0.0905; inn 2 0.0475 / 0.0502 / 0.0876. IPL-only inn 1 0.0386 / 0.0417 / 0.0844; inn 2 0.0415 / 0.0446 / 0.0820. By batter quality tier (Table S5) PP hazard ranges 0.0421–0.0527 and death 0.0823–0.1105. **Mean wicket cost c(s)** by over band: overs 1–6 3.10 runs, 7–10 2.49, 11–15 2.42, 16–20 2.52 (dynamic-programming value function, Bellman recursion). Era multiplier rises to 1.056 (2025) and 1.078 (2026; 2026 PP 1.137). — [arXiv HTML v2](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.18020v2)
- **Ganesh (2026) [preprint, arXiv 2604.13861, submitted to JQAS]**: three-phase player profiles (PP overs 0–5, Middle 6–14, Death 15–19, 0-indexed) over outcomes {W,0,1,2,3,4,6}; Laplace +1 smoothing then James–Stein blend p = λ·p̂_player + (1−λ)·p̄_league-phase with λ = n/(n+50). Data: 1,161 IPL matches 2008–2025 from Cricsheet via yorkr, 273,735 deliveries (~264,800 legal). Example phase economies used: Rashid Khan death ER 8.40 (best); Prasidh Krishna death ER 9.79; Washington Sundar middle ER 7.07 vs death 9.61; Rashid's marginal advantage 1.39 RPO at death over 16 vs 0.49 RPO in middle. — [arXiv HTML v2](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.13861v2)
- **Misra (2026) "The Set-Batter Paradox" [working paper, Columbia]**: within-batter SR lift at balls 30–34 is +41.5 (SE 3.1) vs spin and +34.8 (SE 2.7) vs pace (174,332 pace balls, 97,691 spin balls; bowler style from Cricinfo style codes 1–18 pace, 22–27 spin); at balls 45–49 spin lift +54.4 vs pace +34.6; hazard lift similar (+0.032 spin, +0.036 pace at 30–34). Break-even wicket cost at 30–34: 10.0 runs vs pace, 12.9 vs spin. — [Columbia PDF](https://www.cs.columbia.edu/~misra/set-batter-2026.pdf)
- **Bang, Chakraborty & Chakraborty (2022) [peer-reviewed, minor journal]**, "Do spinners matter in the powerplay of T20? Evidence from IPL", *Sport Science* pp.18–22, IPL 2008–2020: spinners have better powerplay economy than pacers; when defending a total, spinners better on average, economy and combined bowling rate. No numeric values on landing page; PDF unavailable. — [ISI Digital Commons](https://digitalcommons.isical.ac.in/journal-articles/3298)
- **Najdan, Robins & Glazier (2014) [peer-reviewed]**, *Int J Performance Analysis in Sport* 14(1):276–295, 29 winning / 30 losing innings, 2010 English domestic T20: winners lost fewer wickets in PP and overs 7–10; later papers cite it as reporting "a slight disadvantage of bowling spin during the last six overs". Full text 403 (core.ac.uk); the spin claim is second-hand via Kerruish et al. — [IDEAS record](https://ideas.repec.org/a/taf/rpanxx/v14y2014i1p276-295.html)
- Davis, Perera & Swartz (2015): batting outcome probabilities are multiplicative in over × wickets (p_{iowj} ∝ τ_{owj}·p_{i70j}, baseline = 7th over, 0 wickets); authors note a "period of adjustment" in the first two overs and an over × wickets interaction (e.g. batter more aggressive in the 19th over at 2 down than at 9 down). — [SFU PDF](https://www.sfu.ca/~tswartz/papers/t20sim.pdf)

### Inferences
- A reimplementable phase × type scheme: multiply expected runs/ball by Bandyopadhyay's γ^type (Table S2) and γ^scen; multiply dismissal hazard by phase baseline (≈0.045 PP/Mid, ≈0.09 death). Spin's phase advantage is almost entirely a death-overs economy effect (−4 to −5%), while leg-spin is +5.6% expensive in the powerplay — consistent with the Ganjoo/Kerruish picture that wrist spin is a wicket-taking (not run-saving) option.
- For batter pace/spin skill the literature offers no population priors; use per-player phase × type profiles with James–Stein shrinkage toward league phase means (Ganesh's λ = n/(n+50) is a sensible default; Bandyopadhyay's κ values give stronger shrinkage for sparse factors).
- Needs labels: bowler sub-type (RA pace / LA pace / leg-spin / off-spin / SLA) is required for Table S2; batter hand is not.

### Gaps
- Bandyopadhyay does not report type-specific dismissal hazards or batter-handedness interactions.
- No published population estimate of batter strike rate vs pace vs spin by phase (only blog-level plots and per-player news articles).
- Najdan et al. spin-at-death result could not be verified against the paper.
- Swartz-group papers do not model bowler type at all (bowler identity only).

## KQ3. Batting order: optimal vs actual, situational reordering (pinch hitters, steadying after collapse, finishers)

### Takeaway
Simulation studies put the value of batting-order optimisation at roughly +6 runs/innings in ODIs (Swartz et al. 2006) and 15–30 expected-run-differential points in T20 when order is co-optimised with selection and bowling (Perera, Davis & Swartz 2016); a pure "sort by strike rate" reorder lifted a weak team's simulated win% from 21% to 37% (Silva et al. 2016). In-match reordering is formally shown to add "significant" expected score (Norman & Clarke 2010, dynamic programming, no magnitudes) and in a live IPL chase an optimal next-in choice added +4.1 pp win probability (Ganesh 2026). The empirical case for holding a finisher back is weak: Misra (2026) shows a set batter is more dismissable per ball than when fresh but still +54 SR better than the fresh replacement.

### Cited Findings
- **Swartz, Gill, Beaudoin & de Silva (2006) [peer-reviewed]**, *Computers & OR* 33(7):1939–1950. Method: Bayesian log-linear multinomial model for 7 ball outcomes per batter with covariates wickets lost (w/9), balls bowled (b/299) and D/L resource R(w,b)/100 (common slopes, batter-specific intercepts), fitted in WinBUGS on 71 India first innings (Dec 1998–Mar 2003), 404 collapsed multinomials; extras q1=0.0356 (wides/no-balls), q2=0.0208 (byes). Simulated annealing over 4C2·11! ≈ 240M orders: temperatures t=0.5·0.9^k, k=0..14, 100 iterations per plateau (escape after >10 state changes), n sims rising 200, 900, 1600 … 10,000 (SE ≈ 0.5 runs), neighbourhood = randomly permute m=4 consecutive positions, −5 run penalty for infeasible bowler counts; 7.5 h on a Sun workstation. Results: best orders 256.4 and 256.0 expected first-innings runs vs 250.1 for India's 2003 WC final order (**≈ +6 runs**); the *reversed* order yields 201.2 (**−49 runs**); swapping openers has negligible effect; positions 7–11 permutations have little effect. — [SFU PDF](https://www.sfu.ca/~tswartz/papers/optorders.pdf)
- **Perera, Davis & Swartz (2016) [peer-reviewed]**, *J Stat Comput Simul* 86(14):2888–2900. Objective E(D)=E(runs scored)−E(runs allowed) vs an average opponent, 25,000 simulated innings pairs (SE<0.2 runs). SA: each step picks team-swap / adjacent batting swap / bowling-slot swap with prob 1/3 each; double swaps for i≤500; cooling t_i = 0.5·0.9^⌊i/100⌋ (advance plateau after >20 acceptances); N_i = min(25000, 1000+16i); 1,500 iterations ≈ 24 h on a laptop (4 cores). Bowling order represented as a 4·M_B list of bowler symbols (enforces 4-over cap); swaps producing consecutive overs by the same bowler are rejected. Results: India optimal E(D)=32.4 vs actual lineups 2.9 (2010), 7.4 (2012), 1.1 (2014) — a "30-run gap", partly due to selection (M.M. Sharma, Gambhir/Sehwag) and partly order (Dhoni optimal at No. 9 vs actual 4–7); South Africa optimal 36.6 vs 14.7/19.8/14.7 (16.8–21.9 runs better; de Villiers optimal as opener vs actual No. 5); all-star XI E(D)=61.6. Small changes (e.g. swapping 9th/10th) change E(D) by <0.5 runs. — [SFU PDF](https://www.sfu.ca/~tswartz/papers/lineups.pdf)
- **Silva, Perera, Davis & Swartz (2016) "Tactics for Twenty20 cricket" [peer-reviewed]**, *South African Statistical Journal* 50(2):261–271. Wickets matter less in T20: 8th batter reached in 65% of T20I first innings vs 84% ODI; all out 10% vs 29% (243 T20Is, 835 ODIs, 2005–2013); at 90% of overs, ≥6 wickets down in only 24% of T20 innings vs 54% ODI. Re-ordering Bangladesh's Jan 2016 XI strictly by career strike rate (Shakib 132.6 from No. 6 to No. 1; Tamim 117.1 from 1 to 4) raised simulated win% vs India from **21% to 37%** and mean run differential from −22.1 to −10.0. Caveat by authors: position affects how freely a batter can bat, so career SR is an imperfect criterion. Increased aggression (batting as if one fewer wicket down, "WSB −1") raised Bangladesh win% 21.3→22.8% (optimum WSB −0.9) with SD of run diff 28.6→28.9; too much aggression (WSB −5) cut it to 17.1%. For NZ vs India, WSB −1.2 raised win% 59.3→60.2%. Full SA optimisation of NZ lineup for win% raised 59%→70% (Williamson moved from opener to No. 6, Munro from 7 to opener). — [SFU PDF](https://www.sfu.ca/~tswartz/papers/tactics.pdf)
- **Ganesh (2026) [preprint]**: batting MDP with state (runs needed r, balls b, wickets w), terminal 1 if r≤0; action = choose next batter from remaining pool; exhaustive enumeration of n! orders in two passes (3,000 sims each → top 10 re-run at 20,000; final 50,000, MC SE ≤0.22 pp). Case MI vs KKR 29 Mar 2026, 148/1 needing 73 off 44 (RRR 9.95), pool {Suryakumar, Tilak, Hardik, Dhir}: actual order 52.4% (ranked 5th of 6), optimal 56.5% (**+4.1 pp**), worst 50.0%; by next-in: Suryakumar 56.5, Dhir 55.7, Tilak 55.1, Hardik 53.3. Rationale: Suryakumar had lowest middle-over dismissal prob (0.035) and best middle SR among non-death specialists (143.8) → best "bridge"; Dhir (death SR 204, weak middle) should be promoted to 4 so he reaches the death; promoting Dhir worth ≈0.18 runs per extra ball ≈0.92 runs for 5 extra death balls. Author argues win probability, not expected runs, is the right objective in close chases. — [arXiv HTML v2](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.13861v2)
- **Norman & Clarke (2010) [peer-reviewed]**, *JORS* 61(6):980–986, DOI 10.1057/jors.2009.54: dynamic-programming model; "in all forms of cricket, expected score rises significantly when captains allow a variable batting order chosen on the state of the game" (abstract gives no magnitudes). — [IDEAS/RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/pal/jorsoc/v61y2010i6d10.1057_jors.2009.54.html)
- **Norman & Clarke (2003) [peer-reviewed]**, *JORS* 54(8):838–845, night-watchman DP: sending a lower-order batter after a late wicket is "often sub-optimal"; justified only when several recognised and several lower-order batters remain (e.g. 4 and 4), not with 2 and 2 and few overs left. **Norman & Clarke (2007)** *JORS* 58(12):1678–1682 sticky-wicket DP: sending a lower-order batter to "hold the fort" is often optimal only if the difficult conditions persist to the close, rarely if transitory. (Test-cricket analogues of the "steadying batter" question.) — [IFORS abstract](https://iaorifors.com/paper/43488); [Bohrium record](https://www.bohrium.com/paper-details/dynamic-programming-in-cricket-optimizing-batting-order-for-a-sticky-wicket/812054368915292161-6912)
- **Misra (2026) [working paper]** replacement counterfactual: matching set balls (≥20 faced) at state (over, wickets) to fresh balls (≤2 faced) at (over, wickets+1), 100 cells, 66,181 set / 29,938 fresh balls: set batters out-score the replacement by ΔSR = **+54.3 ± 1.5** with Δhazard = +0.022 ± 0.002 per ball; by phase ΔSR +71 (PP), +52 (middle), +60 (death); Δhazard +0.030, +0.025, −0.001. Net at wicket cost 15: +0.41 runs/ball in favour of keeping the set batter. Caveat: ignores captains' bowler response to a wicket. — [Columbia PDF](https://www.cs.columbia.edu/~misra/set-batter-2026.pdf)
- **Date, ESPNcricinfo via ESPN (10 May 2019), IPL chases 2008–Apr 2019 [journalism]**: for RRR≈9 (targets 170–189), successful chasers reached the required rate by over 6 and held it; unsuccessful chasers who had lost ≤3 wickets by over 10 faced 10.9 RPO at over 11 (~2 RPO worse); losing ≥3 wickets in the first 10 overs at RRR 9 happened 32 times with 2 wins; teams >10 runs behind halfway after 10 overs but ≥7 wickets in hand won 143 / lost 175 (n=318); last-10-over chases of 80–100 with ≥7 wickets succeeded 63%, 110 failed 5 of 6. Conclusion: T20 chases are decided in the first ten overs; preserving wickets at the cost of a rising rate is generally ineffective. — [ESPN](https://www.espn.co.uk/cricket/story/_/id/26712889)
- Swartz et al. (2006) discussion explicitly proposes real-time re-optimisation of the remaining order given overs, wickets and batters dismissed (fewer permutations → cheaper). — [SFU PDF](https://www.sfu.ca/~tswartz/papers/optorders.pdf)
- Wikipedia-derived definition of pinch hitter (lower-order quick scorer promoted when runs are needed fast; wicket "not regarded as being so valuable") — [Wikipedia mirror](https://en.wikipedia.com/wiki/Tail-end). ESPNcricinfo "The weird world of T20 pinch hitters" (Apr 2022) argues pinch-hitting has fallen out of favour; Willey used as a "powerplay maximiser" at two down — snippet only, page 403 — [ESPNcricinfo](https://www.espncricinfo.com/story/ipl-2022-the-weird-world-of-t20-pinch-hitters-1309489)

### Inferences
- Scale for a game: a well-chosen vs poorly-chosen order among plausible top-7s is worth single-digit runs (ODI +6; T20 adjacent swaps <0.5 runs; worst-case reversal −20%). Large gains only come from gross misplacement of a high-SR batter (Shakib at 6) or from co-optimising selection and bowling.
- Situational rule supported by Ganesh and Silva et al.: with few overs left and wickets in hand, promote the highest death-phase SR; with many overs left, send the lowest middle-phase dismissal probability ("bridge"). The DP literature (Norman & Clarke) formalises state-dependent orders but gives no T20 magnitudes.
- "Steadying batter after a collapse" has no T20 evidence; Test-cricket DP suggests it is usually wrong unless conditions are persistently bad. Date's chase data suggest wicket-preservation strategies lose.
- Pinch-hitter and collapse-response behaviour must be inferred from Cricsheet (actual entry positions vs listed order); no published frequency statistics located.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed study of how often T20 teams actually promote/demote in-match, or of pinch-hitter success rates (ESPNcricinfo 2022 piece inaccessible).
- Norman & Clarke (2010) magnitudes not available without the paywalled full text.
- Perera et al. (2016) optimal E(D) gains conflate selection, order and bowling; no decomposition of order-only value in T20.

## KQ4. Bowler usage: when captains change bowlers, death specialists, matchup-based changes, MDP/optimisation with the no-consecutive-overs constraint

### Takeaway
Formal treatments exist (Ganesh 2026 MDP with simulated annealing over feasible plans; Perera et al. 2016 SA over bowling orders encoding the 4-over cap and no-consecutive rule; Silva et al. 2016 "bowl in order of economy"), with gains of +5.2 pp defend probability in one IPL case and +3 pp win% / +2 runs from economy-ordering; the consistent finding is that scarce death-capable overs should be spent where their marginal RPO advantage is largest (death), and that phase-agnostic deployment is the common failure. No empirical study of bowler removal after an expensive over was found.

### Cited Findings
- **Ganesh (2026) [preprint]**: bowling MDP state (deficit d, balls b, wickets w); action = feasible bowling plan π over remaining overs; feasible set = quota (4 − overs bowled) and no consecutive overs for the same bowler (removes ~15–20% of quota-valid plans). Duality V^batting + V^bowling = 1 (no draws). SA: 8,000 steps, linear cooling T0=0.05→1e−6, Metropolis acceptance, neighbour = replace one over's bowler respecting quota/consecutive rules; 5,000 sims screening, top 10 re-run at 30,000, final 50,000. Case GT vs PBKS 31 Mar 2026 (PBKS 83/2 needing 80 off 60): actual plan defend prob 39.1%, optimal 44.3% (**+5.2 pp**, z≈19.7); next-best 44.0/43.9/43.5. Diagnosis: Rashid Khan (death ER 8.40) with one over left should bowl death over 16 (1.39 RPO advantage) not middle (0.49); Prasidh Krishna (death ER 9.79) bowled two death overs at an estimated cost ≈2.8 runs; Siraj left two overs unused; Sundar fine in middle (ER 7.07) but not death (9.61). Intro also claims a KKR bowling gap of +18.1 pp but the extracted text lacks supporting results. Author's reading: "observed sub-optimality is consistent with phase-agnostic deployment". — [arXiv HTML v2](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.13861v2)
- **Perera, Davis & Swartz (2016) [peer-reviewed]**: bowling order is a 20-slot assignment drawn from a 4·M_B symbol list (4-over cap); proposals swap a slot with any symbol, rejecting consecutive-over repeats; states "we have not seen any previous work that addresses bowling orders"; India optimal XI used one seamer (B Kumar) and three spinners, challenging the "mix pace and spin" tradition; SA optimal used three seamers. The appendix notes no closed form exists for the count of 20-over sequences with per-bowler caps and no adjacent repeats. — [SFU PDF](https://www.sfu.ca/~tswartz/papers/lineups.pdf)
- **Silva et al. (2016) [peer-reviewed]**: making Bangladesh's five bowlers bowl in order of increasing career economy (M Rahman 6.03, Shakib 7.20, Hossain 7.74, Mortaza 8.46, S Rahman 8.52), four consecutive overs each (acknowledged as rule-breaking/unrealistic), lifts win% 21%→24% and run differential −22.1→−20.1; authors argue T20 bowling should emphasise run prevention over wickets, and that teams with larger economy disparities would gain more. — [SFU PDF](https://www.sfu.ca/~tswartz/papers/tactics.pdf)
- Bandyopadhyay (2026): wicket cost by over band 3.10 (1–6), 2.49 (7–10), 2.42 (11–15), 2.52 (16–20) runs; death-over hazard ≈2× powerplay (0.0905 vs 0.0448 pooled) and death runs/ball +13–19% — the state space a bowling-change model must condition on. — [arXiv HTML v2](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.18020v2)
- **Misra (2026)** references three companion manuscripts: "Five Bowlers in Eighteen Years: The Persistence and Rarity of Death-Over Skill in the IPL", "Pick Which Four: Quota Exhaustion and the Price of Saving the Best Bowler for the 19th Over in the IPL", and "The Unbothered Bowler: Handedness and Partnership Production in Domestic T20" — titles only; not located online (Columbia homepage lists no cricket PDFs; web search returned nothing). — [Columbia PDF, references](https://www.cs.columbia.edu/~misra/set-batter-2026.pdf)
- **Hot hand**: Sornette, Ram & Nandan find individual-level performance clustering in international cricket careers but no team-level or match-outcome predictability — not over-to-over and not T20-specific. — [PMC9270381](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9270381/)
- **Death-over benchmarks [blog/journalism]**: average ≈10.6 RPO in overs 17–20 in 2022 T20Is (Aksay Substack); best T20I death economies since 2016 (min 250 balls) Rashid Khan 7.27, Bumrah 7.39 (Cricket Addictor). — [Aksay Substack](https://aksay.substack.com/p/corridor-of-uncertainty-death-bowling); [Cricket Addictor](https://cricketaddictor.com/editors-picks/best-economy-in-death-overs-in-t20is-since-2016)
- **Epasinghege Dona, Nguyen, Gill & Swartz (2022) [peer-reviewed]**, "Expected economy rate", *Studies of Applied Economics* 40(1): proof-of-concept xG-style expected runs per ball from ball-tracking features using random forests (Cricket Australia data) — relevant as a quality-adjusted economy baseline, not a usage study. — [SFU PDF](https://www.sfu.ca/~tswartz/papers/xeconrate.pdf)

### Inferences
- Reimplementable rule set from Ganesh/Perera: enumerate or anneal over remaining-over assignments subject to (i) ≤4 overs each, (ii) no consecutive overs, (iii) objective = defend probability via the batting-side value function; a cheaper heuristic that matches the paper's optimal plans is "assign each bowler's remaining overs to the phase where his (ER_league_phase − ER_own_phase) is largest, death first".
- A "don't bowl a non-death bowler at the death" penalty of ≈1.4 runs/over (Krishna 2.8 runs over two overs; Rashid 1.39 RPO advantage) is the only published magnitude for matchup-by-phase bowling changes.
- Bowler-type labels are needed only for the phase multipliers (KQ2); the MDP/SA machinery itself uses per-bowler phase profiles.

### Gaps
- No empirical study of bowler removal after an expensive over, of captains' actual change frequencies, or of over-to-over economy persistence in T20 (only general hot-hand work).
- No peer-reviewed "death specialist" study; Misra's companion manuscripts (persistence of death skill; quota exhaustion / saving the best bowler for the 19th) could not be located.
- Ganesh's results rest on two 2026 matches and a single-author preprint.

## KQ5. Eye-in / new-batter effects in T20 (early dismissal hazard, "set batter" bonus)

### Takeaway
On 272,029 legal IPL balls (2008–2026), pooled SR rises from 103 (balls 0–4) to 150 (balls 30–34) and 204 (60+); within-batter, within-over, the causal "getting set" SR lift is ≈+37 runs/100 balls at balls 30–34 — but dismissal hazard also rises within batter (+0.034 per ball at 30–34, ≈2× the 0.041 baseline), so there is no "safer when set" effect; the set-batter bonus exists only relative to the weaker replacement (+54 SR, +0.022 hazard). The first ~5 balls are the *lowest*-hazard, lowest-SR state (hazard 0.0406/ball, SR 103).

### Cited Findings
- **Misra (2026) [working paper, Columbia, Apr 2026]**: 281,256 IPL deliveries, 1,183 matches, 19 seasons; 272,029 legal balls, 714 batters, 2,887 batter-seasons; mean 15.2 legal balls per batter-innings (median 11, max 73); pooled SR 132.3; pooled hazard 0.047/ball (1 in 21.3). Method: OLS with batter-season fixed effects and over-number dummies, balls-faced in 5-ball buckets (reference 0–4), cluster-robust SE by batter; decomposition naive − batterFE = compositional, batterFE − (batter+over FE) = match-state, residual = within-batter. — [Columbia PDF](https://www.cs.columbia.edu/~misra/set-batter-2026.pdf)
- Misra **naive pooled hazard by balls faced (Table 1)**: 0–4: 78,225 balls, 0.0406; 5–9: 0.0478; 10–14: 0.0467; 15–19: 0.0484; 20–24: 0.0461; 25–29: 0.0504; 30–34: 0.0543; 35–39: 0.0560; 40–44: 0.0621; 45–49: 0.0657; 50–54: 0.0700; 55–59: 0.0801; 60+: 0.0898. **Pooled SR**: 103.2 (0–4) → 150.2 (30–34) → 203.5 (60+). — [Columbia PDF](https://www.cs.columbia.edu/~misra/set-batter-2026.pdf)
- Misra **SR decomposition (Table 2, runs/100 balls vs 0–4)**: naive / batter-FE / batter+over-FE — 5–9: 30.9/30.4/25.7; 10–14: 40.6/40.4/34.5; 15–19: 41.5/41.9/37.7; 20–24: 40.0/40.9/36.8; 30–34: 47.1/49.2/36.8 (match-state share 0.26, residual 0.78); 40–44: 65.9/70.7/43.2; 60+: 100.4/98.9/29.9 (match-state share 0.69). Compositional share ≈0 (slightly negative). — [Columbia PDF](https://www.cs.columbia.edu/~misra/set-batter-2026.pdf)
- Misra **hazard decomposition (Table 3, dismissals/ball vs 0–4)**: naive / batter-FE / batter+over-FE — 5–9: 0.0073/0.0169/0.0165; 10–14: 0.0062/0.0209/0.0204; 20–24: 0.0055/0.0261/0.0254; 30–34: 0.0137/0.0385/0.0337; 45–49: 0.0251/0.0562/0.0346; 60+: 0.0493/0.0857/0.0369. Adding cumulative-wickets dummies barely moves it (30–34: SR +36.3, hazard +0.0327). Break-even wicket cost w* = (SR lift/100)/(hazard lift): min 8.1 runs (60+), max 16.9 (10–14), ≤12.1 for every bucket ≥25 balls → net per-ball value negative at any wicket cost >≈12 runs. Recent 10-ball SR does not predict next-ball hazard within batter (coef −1.5e−5, SE 1.8e−5): "protect mode" buys no safety. — [Columbia PDF](https://www.cs.columbia.edu/~misra/set-batter-2026.pdf)
- Misra Kohli case (6,575 balls, 261 innings): SR 118 vs pool 116 at 0–9; 129 vs 145 at 10–29; 165 vs 160 at 30–49; 211 vs 187 at 50+; hazard below pool at every bucket (−0.019 at 0–9); continuing average SR/hazard falls 47.1 → 42.7 → 35.1 → 34.4. — [Columbia PDF](https://www.cs.columbia.edu/~misra/set-batter-2026.pdf)
- **ESPNcricinfo "Do teams benefit when their batters play long innings in T20s?" [journalism, 403]**: search snippets report ≈589,000 balls, "a new batter ramps up to a 'stable' strike rate in about ten balls", the gap between stable and starting rate being the new-batter cost; losing a wicket costs more at the death, with a comparable loss for the first two powerplay wickets because batters are slower to settle early. Snippet-only. — [ESPNcricinfo 1324594](https://www.espncricinfo.com/story/do-teams-benefit-when-their-batters-play-long-innings-in-t20s-1324594)
- **ESPNcricinfo "How much do wickets matter in Twenty20 cricket?" [journalism, 403]**: snippet reports early IPL wickets worth ≈2.5 runs each, with two mechanisms — fewer wickets in hand → slower batting for the rest of the innings, and the new batter's eye-in short-term dip. — [ESPNcricinfo 612432](https://espncricinfo.com/story/how-much-do-wickets-matter-in-twenty20-cricket-612432)
- Bandyopadhyay (2026) estimates a "set curve" (Table S8) in his value-function transition model but the extracted text gives no numbers; Finding: wicket value peaks in the powerplay; mean wicket cost 3.10 runs in overs 1–6 vs ≈2.5 later. — [arXiv HTML v2](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.18020v2)
- **Brewer (2008) arXiv 0801.4408 [preprint]** "Getting your eye in: a Bayesian analysis of early dismissals in cricket" — hazard elevated at the start of an innings; Test/first-class data, not T20. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/0801.4408)
- Davis, Perera & Swartz (2015): data show "the first two overs … a period of adjustment to the bowler, to the ball, to the pitch" at innings level (not per batter). — [SFU PDF](https://www.sfu.ca/~tswartz/papers/t20sim.pdf)

### Inferences
- For a T20 engine: model a new batter with SR multiplier ≈0.78 for balls 0–4 and ≈0.95 for balls 5–9 relative to his steady state (from 103→132 pooled, ≈+31 SR lift by 5–9), reaching steady SR by ~ball 10–15; but do *not* lower his hazard once set — if anything hazard should drift up ≈+0.003/ball per 5 balls faced. A "set batter is safer" rule is contradicted by the only large-sample T20 analysis.
- The "keep the set batter / hold the finisher back" question is really about replacement quality; Misra's +54 SR, +0.022 hazard replacement delta is the right counterfactual to encode.
- Misra's pace/spin split (KQ2) implies the eye-in SR curve should be steeper vs spin.

### Gaps
- Misra (2026) is a single-author working paper (acknowledges analysis "performed with Claude Code"), not peer-reviewed; no replication in T20Is or other leagues.
- ESPNcricinfo ~10-ball settling estimate is snippet-only; underlying tables inaccessible.
- No published per-ball hazard for balls 1, 2, 3 individually (Misra buckets 0–4).

## KQ6. Left-right batting combinations

### Takeaway
The largest study (Stellenbosch WP05/2026, 96,000 international partnerships, 3.4M balls) finds the mixed-hand run-rate premium is "precisely zero" in Tests, ODIs and T20Is after controlling for batter quality and match-innings fixed effects, with higher per-ball dismissal risk for mixed pairs; the raw premium is composition (left-handers are better at international level). Journalism on ODIs agrees (Kimber 2019: +3 SR points vs spin only; wides more frequent with left-handers). T20I quantile regressions show a "partial exception" of positive effects, unquantified.

### Cited Findings
- **Stellenbosch Univ. Dept of Economics WP05/2026 (rev. Mar 2026), "Invisible Handedness: The Myth of Left-Right Batting Partnerships" [working paper]**: ~96,000 partnerships, 3.4M deliveries, all men's internationals (Tests, ODIs, T20Is); controls for batsman quality, match-innings fixed effects; ball-level mechanism tests, randomisation inference, Oaxaca-Blinder decomposition, quantile regressions (25/50/75th pct, T20Is). Results: mixed-hand premium "precisely zero in all three formats" and for every partnership-level scoring margin; per-ball dismissal risk higher for mixed-hand pairs, offsetting any scoring edge; raw descriptive premium "largely explained" by superior quality of left-handed batsmen; strike rotation imposes a universal switching cost on bowlers that does not interact with hand composition; T20I quantile regressions detect positive effects across the IQR — a "partial exception" needing domestic-league samples. No coefficients on landing page. — [Stellenbosch WP](https://www.ekon.sun.ac.za/wpapers/2026/wp052026)
- **Kimber (ESPN, 2 Jul 2019), ODI data by Shiva Jayaraman [journalism]**: one left-hander in the pair produces a wide about every 6.4 balls more often than two right-handers, a second adds ≈5.2; left-handers face 34% of ODI balls; left-right pairs gain only ≈3 SR points vs spin, and right-right pairs average slightly better; right-right facing seam average under 30 (only case where pairing changes the average); conclusion: splitting same-hand pairs worthwhile only when the opposition has two spinners turning the ball the same way. — [ESPN](https://www.espn.co.uk/cricket/story/_/id/27102343/do-right-left-pairs-crease-work-complicated)
- **Date, ESPNcricinfo (ODI partnerships since 1979) [journalism, 403]**: snippet — left-right pairs have no systematic advantage; bowlers least accurate when two left-handers bat. — [ESPNcricinfo print 826753](https://www.espncricinfo.com/print/story/826753)
- **ACS Cricket Statistician No. 202 [hobbyist statistics]**: among the 200 most prolific Test partnerships, differences between left-right and same-hand pairs were small; "no evidence that left-right pairs make it more difficult for the bowlers". — [ACS archive](https://archive.acscricket.com/journals/202/35/)
- **Valero & Swartz (2012) [peer-reviewed]**, *Sri Lankan J Applied Statistics* 13:87–98, ODI opening-partnership "synergy": performance with common partner vs other partners — mean benefit 4.7% ≈ 1.7 runs on an average 36.2-run stand, one-sided t-test p=0.069; sign test p-value not significant; conclusion "synergies … nothing more than a sporting myth"; authors suggest repeating in T20. — [SFU PDF](https://www.sfu.ca/~tswartz/papers/juan.pdf)
- Kerruish et al. (2025) imply the theoretical mechanism: a pair split by hand forces one of two same-direction spinners into a turning-in match-up, which concedes ≈30–40% higher run odds (LHB vs leg-spin/SLA ORs 1.30–1.42 relative to off-spin). — [PMC11985319](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11985319)

### Inferences
- A generic left-right partnership bonus is not supported; any effect should be implemented as the matchup consequence (KQ1): a mixed pair guarantees that a single-direction spinner faces one unfavourable match-up per over, which is where Kimber's "+3 SR vs spin" comes from.
- A small wides uplift when a left-hander is batting (Kimber's ODI framing: one left-hander means a wide roughly every 6.4 balls more often than two right-handers) is the only consistently reported handedness-mixing effect; bowlers' accuracy, not partnership scoring, is what mixing perturbs.

### Gaps
- Stellenbosch coefficients, SEs and the T20I quantile magnitudes are not on the landing page (PDF not fetched).
- No franchise-T20 (IPL/BBL) study of left-right pairs; Misra's "Unbothered Bowler: Handedness and Partnership Production in Domestic T20" exists only as a reference.
- ESPNcricinfo Date ODI analysis inaccessible.
