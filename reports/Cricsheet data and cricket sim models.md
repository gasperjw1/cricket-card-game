# Cricsheet Can Power Your Management Sim Today

Cricsheet gives you everything a ball-by-ball management engine needs except player biographies: **23,080 matches and roughly 11.7 million deliveries** of free JSON (147.6 MB zipped) covering every men's and women's international since 2001–2003 and some 40 franchise and domestic competitions, and since **7 October 2026 it is explicitly licensed under ODC-By 1.0**, an attribution-only licence with no non-commercial clause and no share-alike, so a free hobby game and any later paid version are both permitted provided you credit Cricsheet and name the licence. The licence covers the database only; it says nothing about using real player names, which is a separate publicity-rights question the research did not resolve and which every commercial cricket manager simply accepts. The academic literature on turning this data into a simulator is small, mature and convergent: a per-delivery multinomial over {0, 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, wicket} whose probabilities are a shrunk per-batter baseline, scaled by a shared (over × wickets) situation multiplier, blended with a bowler vector, with a separate Bernoulli for extras. Simon Fraser's Swartz group published this structure with worked parameter tables; Canterbury's Brooker published every ordered-probit coefficient for ODIs; Auckland's Stevenson and Brewer published the "getting your eye in" hazard curve; and three 2026 preprints reproduce the whole thing from Cricsheet with shrinkage priors of 40–60 balls. Practitioners, from Cricket Captain to Andrew Kuo's neural simulator, land on the same architecture and the same lesson: per-ball models recover realistic score distributions easily, but equal sides stay near coin-flip and the fun comes from legible levers (aggression, bowling changes, a visible win predictor), not from accuracy. The recommendation is to build a Cricsheet-to-SQLite aggregation pipeline keyed on Cricsheet player IDs, implement the Davis–Perera–Swartz T20 engine with Ganesh-style phase shrinkage first, bolt on the Stevenson–Brewer eye-in multiplier and a Delta-style par-score table, and defer Tests, fielding matrices and ageing until the T20 engine passes five published realism checks.

## Cricsheet delivers 11.7 million deliveries under an attribution-only licence

The JSON format (v1.3.0) is the only one worth building on. Per delivery it records batter, bowler, non-striker, runs split into batter/extras/total with a `non_boundary` flag, typed extras (byes, leg-byes, no-balls, wides, penalty), a `wickets` array with kind, player out and fielders, DRS `review` outcomes and concussion/other `replacements`; per match it records dates, venue, toss, outcome with method and margin, officials, event and stage, team player lists, powerplay windows, chase targets and declared/forfeited/super-over flags, plus a `registry` mapping every name in the file to an 8-character Cricsheet person ID ([Cricsheet JSON format](https://cricsheet.org/format/json/)). Direct inspection of all 1,243 IPL files confirmed the fields are populated at scale: **295,732 deliveries, 14,705 wickets, 16,227 extras, 990 reviews**, with wicket kinds distributed as caught 9,321, bowled 2,460, run out 1,194, lbw 883, caught-and-bowled 410, stumped 388 ([ipl_json.zip](https://cricsheet.org/downloads/ipl_json.zip)). The flattened "Ashwin" CSV is adequate for basic aggregates but drops reviews, replacements, powerplays, targets and the non-boundary flag ([Cricsheet CSV Ashwin format](https://cricsheet.org/format/csv_ashwin/)); YAML is deprecated and XML is a third-party conversion ([Cricsheet formats](https://cricsheet.org/format/)).

Coverage is broad where your roster lives. The site holds **919 Tests, 3,203 ODIs, 5,775 T20Is, 110 countries and 298 club teams**, with IPL 1,243 matches, T20 Blast 1,570, BBL 662, PSL 357, CPL 445, The Hundred 389, SA20 130 and the full IPL 2026 season already present ([Cricsheet home](https://cricsheet.org/); [Cricsheet downloads](https://cricsheet.org/downloads/)). Effective start dates are Tests from late 2001, ODIs from mid-2002, T20Is from 2005 and domestic T20 from 2008, so career aggregates for older players are truncated and "all-time leaderboards are not comparable to official ICC records" ([cricket-mcp README](https://github.com/mavaali/cricket-mcp)). Two coverage holes matter specifically for Swipe Sixer's 18-nation roster. First, **379 matches involving the Afghanistan men's team are withheld** as a protest over the ICC's treatment of Afghan women's cricket, with no restoration date ([Cricsheet article](https://cricsheet.org/article/explanation-for-withholding-of-afghanistani-matches/)); Rashid Khan, Mujeeb and Nabi keep their IPL, BBL and CPL deliveries but lose their international records, and every other nation's head-to-head against Afghanistan vanishes too. Second, your six associate nations (Nepal, Hong Kong, Scotland, USA, Netherlands, Namibia) exist in the data but with thin samples, which is exactly the regime where the shrinkage methods in the next section stop being optional.

The licence is the headline change. Until this month Cricsheet had never stated one; the maintainer's 7 October 2026 post reads "One major oversight of this project is that I've never previously indicated the license" and places all match data except XML under the **Open Data Commons Attribution License v1.0**, with `LICENSE.txt` now shipped inside every zip ([Cricsheet article, 7 Oct 2026](http://cricsheet.org/article/licensing-and-venues-registry/); [Cricsheet licensing](https://cricsheet.org/licensing/)). ODC-By grants the rights to share, to "produce works from the database" and to "modify, transform and build upon the database", conditioned only on attributing "any public use of the database, or works produced from the database" and making the licence clear ([ODC-By summary](https://opendatacommons.org/licenses/by/summary/)). There is no non-commercial restriction and no share-alike, so derived rating tables can be released under any terms; a published Delta paper built on the same data describes it as "a free-to-use license" ([delta-2026.pdf](https://www.cs.columbia.edu/~misra/delta-2026.pdf)). Two caveats are real. The bundled LICENSE.txt states that it "only governs the rights over the Database, and not the contents of the Database individually" and flags that privacy and data-protection rights may apply to contents ([ipl_json.zip](https://cricsheet.org/downloads/ipl_json.zip)); using real player names in a game is therefore a personality-rights question that Cricsheet cannot license and that none of the four research notes found a legal source on. The project is also a one-person operation run by Stephen Rushe since 2009 with no stated funding and an admitted willingness to make editorial withholdings ([About Cricsheet](https://cricsheet.org/about/)), so a pipeline should cache its own snapshot rather than depend on the site being up.

What Cricsheet does not contain is as important as what it does. The spec has **no ball speed, line, length, shot type, wagon-wheel coordinates, field placements, batting hand or bowling style** ([Cricsheet JSON format](https://cricsheet.org/format/json/); [cricket-mcp README](https://github.com/mavaali/cricket-mcp)). Fielder attribution is complete for catches in IPL data but **15.2% of run-outs (182 of 1,194) name no fielder** ([ipl_json.zip](https://cricsheet.org/downloads/ipl_json.zip)). The people register partly fills the biography gap: `people.csv` holds 18,623 people with a Cricinfo key on 18,578 of them (99.8%), while Cricbuzz keys exist for only 50 ([Cricsheet register](https://cricsheet.org/register/); [people.csv](https://cricsheet.org/register/people.csv)). The cleanest legal bridge to date of birth, handedness and bowling type is the `player_meta` snapshot bundled in Rob Hyndman's GPL-3 `cricketdata` R package, "all players on ESPNCricinfo as at 24 March 2025", keyed by the same Cricinfo ID ([cricketdata reference](https://pkg.robjhyndman.com/cricketdata/reference/fetch_player_meta.html); [CRAN cricketdata](https://cran.r-project.org/package=cricketdata)); scraping Cricinfo yourself is prohibited by Disney's Terms of Use, which forbid "data mining or web scraping" of ESPN properties ([Disney Terms of Use](https://disneytermsofuse.com/english/)), and Wikidata has bowling style for only 1,157 of its 31,715 Cricinfo-linked people ([Wikidata Query Service](https://query.wikidata.org/)). For a 332-card roster this gap is nearly moot: your cards already carry hand-assigned `Handedness` and bowling `Adjective` fields, and maintaining those by hand for a few hundred players is cheaper than any join.

## Per-ball multinomials with a shared situation multiplier are the proven engine skeleton

Every serious academic simulator since 2006 is the same object with different estimation machinery: a categorical distribution over per-ball outcomes conditioned on who is batting, who is bowling and where the innings stands. Swartz, Gill, Beaudoin and de Silva (2006) set the template for ODIs with a seven-category multinomial whose log-odds are a batter baseline plus common slopes on wickets lost, balls bowled and Duckworth–Lewis resources used, with wides/no-balls (rate **0.0356 per ball**) and byes/leg-byes (**0.0208**) drawn as separate Bernoullis outside the multinomial; the paper publishes the full fitted table for 13 Indian batters, with Tendulkar's baseline log-odds (4.15, 2.56, 1.40, −0.26, 2.12, −1.41) against dismissal as the reference category ([optorders.pdf](https://www.sfu.ca/~tswartz/papers/optorders.pdf)). The same team's T20 simulator is the production design to copy. Davis, Perera and Swartz (2015) model batter *i* in over *o* with *w* wickets down as **p_iowj ∝ τ_owj · p_i70j**: a per-batter baseline vector at the reference state "over 7, no wickets down" multiplied by a situation factor τ shared by all batters, then renormalised; bowlers get a symmetric vector q describing them against an average batter, and the batter-versus-bowler distribution is **p + q − p̄** where p̄ is the stage average ([t20sim.pdf](https://www.sfu.ca/~tswartz/papers/t20sim.pdf)). The published Table 3 is a ready sanity target: an average T20I batter after the powerplay should score about **7.5 runs per over at a 3.7% per-ball dismissal rate**, rising to roughly 7.8 runs per over and 10.7% dismissal at over 15 with two down, while Shane Watson at the same reference state runs at 9.2 per over with 9.4% sixes ([t20sim.pdf](https://www.sfu.ca/~tswartz/papers/t20sim.pdf)). Observed T20I first-innings proportions over 2005–2013 were 0.301 dots, 0.411 singles, 0.078 twos, 0.006 threes, 0.103 fours, 0.042 sixes and 0.057 wickets, against Test fourth-innings proportions of 0.743 dots and 0.003 sixes, a fourteen-fold difference in six frequency that tells you the engine needs a format-specific τ, not just a format-specific scoring multiplier ([t20sim.pdf](https://www.sfu.ca/~tswartz/papers/t20sim.pdf)).

The 2015 paper's details are what make it implementable without MCMC. Because few batters have batted in both the first over and the nineteenth, τ is estimated by chaining adjacent-state ratios (over *o* to *o*+1, wicket *w* to *w*+1) averaged across batters with inverse-variance weights and smoothed; the baselines are then shrunk toward the population mean with a Dirichlet prior of sample size **c = 60**, chosen because more than 200 of 384 batters had faced fewer than 40 balls; recency enters by weighting each past match's counts by **0.88^g** where *g* is games ago; home advantage is a flat ×1.03/×0.97 on runs; and a chase is handled by comparing the batter's expected runs per resource consumed against (target − score + 1)/resources remaining and shifting him to a later-over column of τ until he is aggressive enough, with a 0.8 damping factor reproducing real teams' tendency to accelerate late ([t20sim.pdf](https://www.sfu.ca/~tswartz/papers/t20sim.pdf)). With 2.7 million Cricsheet T20 deliveries now available (versus the 30,458 the paper had), τ can be estimated directly from cell frequencies instead of the chaining trick, which removes the one piece of the method the authors never tabulated.

Brooker's 2010 Canterbury thesis, the ancestor of WASP, is the alternative ODI design and the only one that publishes every coefficient. It solves a backward-induction value function **V(i,j) = E[r_ij] + ω_ij·V(i+1,j+1) + (1−ω_ij)·V(i+1,j) + extras term** over ball *i* and wickets *j*, with runs from an ordered probit on {0,1,2,3,4,6}, wickets from a probit, and extras from a probit plus ordered probit, all with kinks at ball 48 (new-ball settling) and ball 240 (death), fitted to about 91,000 legal deliveries from 311 ODIs; Appendix C lists every intercept and slope, and the fit error against observed expected-remaining-runs is **0.66–7.23 runs** by wickets lost, at worst 3% of a first-innings total ([Brooker thesis](https://www.cmi.ac.in/~rlk/Cricket_Analytics/AuxiliaryMaterial/AnEconomicAnalysisofAbilityStrategyandFairnessinODICricket.pdf)). This is a complete ODI per-ball generator for 2001–2008 scoring levels; the 2006 and 2010 parameter sets both predate the T20-era inflation that forced the Stern revision of D/L, so an era multiplier is mandatory if you reuse them. The D/L curve itself, Z(u,w) = Z0(w)(1 − e^{−b(w)u}), is a legitimate calibration target even though the official constants are secret: the Standard Edition table is public (20 overs and 10 wickets in hand is 56.6% of resources; 10 overs and 2 wickets is 11.4%) and an unofficial reverse-fit gives Z0(0) = 134.1, b(0) = 0.0274 down to Z0(9) = 4.7, b(9) = 0.763 ([Wikipedia DLS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Duckworth%E2%80%93Lewis%E2%80%93Stern_method); [Flying Colours Maths](https://blog.flyingcoloursmaths.co.uk/red-rag-duckworth-lewis-stern)).

Two within-innings effects deserve a line of code each. Brewer (2008) and Stevenson and Brewer (2017) model a Test batter's dismissal hazard per run as **H(x) = 1/(μ(x)+1)** with an effective average μ(x) = μ₂ + (μ₁ − μ₂)e^{−x/L} that rises from a fresh value μ₁ to a set value μ₂; the fitted values for Lara are μ₁ = 15.1, μ₂ = 61.8, L = 6.1 and for Steve Waugh 13.2, 58.5, 3.1, and the headline finding is that "most players begin their innings playing with between only a quarter and half of their potential batting ability", with log Bayes factors of 1 to 25 over a constant hazard ([arXiv 1609.04078](https://arxiv.org/abs/1609.04078); [arXiv 0801.4408](https://arxiv.org/abs/0801.4408)). The 2021 extension adds a Gaussian process on log μ₂ across the career and multiplicative home (**+17%**) and first-versus-second-team-innings (**+20%**) effects, the latter being the only published estimate of pitch deterioration ([arXiv 1908.11490](https://arxiv.org/abs/1908.11490)). For Tests specifically, Perera, Gill and Swartz (2014) supply a parameter-complete third/fourth-innings model: fourth-innings per-ball proportions, geometric per-over wicket rates by partnership number (r₁..r₁₀ = .108, .079, .076, .081, .077, .094, .131, .166, .179, .212 for an average side, with the opening stand falling fastest because of the new ball), and runs-given-overs means of 169.6 at 50 overs and 316.9 at 100, plus an optimal-declaration table ([declare.pdf](https://www.sfu.ca/~tswartz/papers/declare.pdf)).

The sharpest recent warning comes from a 2026 preprint by Mishra: an exact backward-induction chase model matched per-ball outcome marginals within total variation 0.02 at every required rate and was still **systematically miscalibrated on win probability**, because real innings carry 3–5-ball run-scoring persistence and innings-level heterogeneity that a state-only Markov chain cannot produce; a block bootstrap recovered only 26% of the gap ([arXiv 2608.14696](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.14696)). The practical implication for a game is that a pure per-ball multinomial will be under-dispersed, generating fewer collapses and fewer surges than reality, and needs an innings-level random effect (pitch, day) or short momentum term to feel like cricket.

## Shrinkage toward a 50–60-ball prior turns sparse stats into ratings

The question of how to convert raw aggregates into a per-player number splits into two families: ordinal indices meant for ranking humans, and probability-scale parameters meant for a simulator. Only the second family drives a ball-by-ball engine, but the first supplies three primitives worth stealing. The ICC system, designed by Rob Eastaway and Gordon Vince, is an exponentially decayed average of per-match performance scores on a 0–1000 scale, adjusted for opposition strength (circularly, batters against bowler ratings and vice versa), match scoring level and result; its new-player damping gives a Test batter 70% of computed rating at 10 innings and 100% at 40, and it docks 1%, 0.5% and 2% per missed Test, ODI and T20I respectively, but the actual coefficients and decay constant are unpublished ([Plus Magazine](https://plus.maths.org/howzat); [MRF Tyres ICC Player Rankings](https://www.icc-cricket.com/news/mrf-tyres-icc-player-rankings)). Lemmer's academic indices add match-relative scaling, adjusting a batter's runs by **(strike rate / match global strike rate)^0.5** so that 60 off 50 on a 60-per-100 pitch is worth more than on a 95 pitch, and Bhattacharjee and Dhar's format fit shows that in T20 bowling **economy should outweigh average by roughly 6:1 (α = 0.14) versus 7:3 in ODIs (α = 0.30)** when matching ICC top-20 lists ([Lemmer, JSSM 2011](https://www.jssm.org/jssm-10-630.xml-Fulltext); [Bhattacharjee & Dhar 2024](https://www.banglajol.info/index.php/ijss/article/view/78223/51271)). Bandyopadhyay's 2026 decomposition independently finds that a bowler's wicket-taking value and run-saving value correlate at only **0.09**, which is a strong argument for keeping "strike" and "economy" as separate bowler parameters rather than one composite ([arXiv 2608.18020](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.18020v2)).

For the simulator family, the recurring design choice is how hard to shrink a player's empirical outcome vector toward a population mean, and four independent sources converge on the same answer. Davis, Perera and Swartz chose a Dirichlet prior sample size of 60 balls ([t20sim.pdf](https://www.sfu.ca/~tswartz/papers/t20sim.pdf)); Ganesh (2026), working from Cricsheet IPL 2008–2025 (1,161 matches, 273,735 deliveries), blends each player's per-phase vector with the phase mean using **λ = n/(n+50)** after Laplace smoothing, with phases defined as overs 1–6, 7–15 and 16–20 ([arXiv 2604.13861](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.13861)); Bandyopadhyay shrinks scoring rate with a pseudo-count of **60 balls and dismissal hazard with 40** ([arXiv 2608.18020](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.18020v2)). The deeper Bayesian machinery exists if you want it: Boys and Philipson's zero-inflated negative-binomial model of Test batting with a per-player quadratic ageing curve, hierarchical normal ability prior and ~11% duck inflation ([arXiv 1806.05496](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1806.05496)), and Stevenson and Brewer's log-normal prior with median 25 on the set average ([ar5iv 1609.04078](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1609.04078)). But the practical lesson is that a pseudo-count of about 50 balls per phase, with the shrinkage target chosen sensibly, gets you most of the way, and that the right target is often not the global mean but an archetype centroid: several analyses k-means-cluster batters on their dot/1/2/4/6/out shares into four to six roles (steady opener, accumulator, finisher, "man-beast") with minimum samples of 50 to 1,000 balls ([Criconometrics](https://criconometrics.substack.com/p/balanced-lineups); [NHSJS 2026](https://nhsjs.com/2026/batting-archetypes-and-team-success-analyzing-the-role-composition-of-teams-in-indian-premier-league-using-machine-learning/)), and those outcome-share vectors are literally the multinomial parameters the engine samples from.

Context adjustment is where the field has moved fastest, and the two 2026 methods are both reproducible from Cricsheet. Misra and Rajaraman's Delta credits each ball with the change in projected total, **T_k = s_k + g(120 − b_k, 10 − w_k)**, where g is simply the empirical mean of (final total − current score) over all first-innings observations of that (balls, wickets) state in IPL 2022–2025; their sanity values are g(120,10) = 183.2, g(60,7) = 96.8, g(24,4) = 40.4 and g(6,3) = 11.3, and opposition quality is handled by iterating per-player average deltas to a fixpoint that stabilised within 0.07 runs per ball ([delta-2026.pdf](https://www.cs.columbia.edu/~misra/delta-2026.pdf)). Bandyopadhyay's RAE/DAR model is the complete version: expected runs per legal ball are **μ₀ = 1.2425 times multipliers for phase×innings, era×phase, phase×wickets, phase×bowler-type, venue×3-year bucket and opposition identity**, fitted by iterated backfitting on 2,727,420 deliveries, and the deviance ranking tells you which covariates matter in T20: **venue (41,767) > wickets (27,972) > opposition bowler (27,545) > phase scenario (21,686) > era (8,611) > bowler type (2,812)**, with ball length adding 41,924 on the tracked subset you cannot get ([arXiv 2608.18020](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.18020v2)). The published bowler-type multipliers are directly usable in lieu of per-batter pace/spin skills, for which no shrunk academic estimate exists: leg-spin runs at 1.056× in the powerplay but 0.950× at the death, right-arm pace at 1.016× rising to 1.033× ([arXiv 2608.18020](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.18020v2)).

Form and age are smaller effects than intuition suggests. Stevenson and Brewer's leave-one-out test on 913 Test batters found a 25%-recent-window average (MSE 588.4) barely beat the career average (589.1) and a 10% window was worse (633.1), with "little evidence for strong short-term form effects for most players" ([ar5iv 1908.11490](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1908.11490)); the Swartz group's current-form and career ratings correlate at 0.77 for batters and 0.91 for bowlers ([moneyball.pdf](https://www.sfu.ca/~tswartz/papers/moneyball.pdf)); and Delta's authors observe that breakout bowlers "regress sharply" the following season ([delta-2026.pdf](https://www.cs.columbia.edu/~misra/delta-2026.pdf)). The published per-match decay constants run from 0.88 (Swartz, posterior-maximised) to the 0.96 and 0.90 illustrations Eastaway uses for the ICC ([t20sim.pdf](https://www.sfu.ca/~tswartz/papers/t20sim.pdf); [Plus Magazine](https://plus.maths.org/howzat)). Ageing is consistently quadratic with batters peaking around **27–30 and ODI bowlers 22–26** by wickets, Boys and Philipson's posterior peaks for Bradman, Smith, Tendulkar and Root all landing between 27.2 and 28.6, and Kent's analyst Dan Weston's working rule being 28–31 ([Frontiers 10.3389/frbhe.2024.1492735](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/behavioral-economics/articles/10.3389/frbhe.2024.1492735/full); [arXiv 1806.05496](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1806.05496); [Dan's Newsletter](https://danweston.substack.com/p/whats-gone-wrong-with-england)). No source quantifies post-peak decline per year, so a career mode's decline slope will be a design choice, not a measurement.

## Commercial managers hide their formula but converge on the same levers

Nothing in the practitioner world contradicts the academic skeleton; it just adds the dials players actually touch. Cricket Captain's engine began as Chris Child's 1992 Birmingham dissertation project and exposes only batting, bowling, fielding, aggression and opener type in its editor ([Giant Bomb](https://giantbomb.com/wiki/Games/International_Cricket_Captain); [Steam](https://store.steampowered.com/app/3757420/)). The most detailed developer disclosure found is the 2022 aggression rework: "All aggression rates are now determined directly from data using a player's strike rate", banded for first-class cricket at >63% very aggressive, 56–63 aggressive, 50–56 normal, 45–50 defensive, <45 very defensive, with limited-overs aggression a weighted average of OD and T20 strike rates relative to format means of 83% and 126% ([Childish Things aggression thread](https://www.childishthings.com/forum/forum/cricket-captain-2022/78888-new-aggression-rating-system-explained)). The 2024 edition was tuned "simulating thousands of matches from every situation" and ships a 7,500-player database with "real-world player form" and an in-match score predictor ([Childish Things CC24 features](https://www.childishthings.com/forum/forum/current-and-future-games/cricket-captain-2024/82523-cricket-captain-2024-release-date-and-features)); the developers also admit a fringe player's "ability can vary significantly between saves", which is a cheap per-save noise injection for replay variety ([CC24 database feedback](https://www.childishthings.com/forum/forum/current-and-future-games/cricket-captain-2024/82879-cc24-database-feedback)). The per-ball resolution formula itself has never been described publicly. Browser managers show the attribute consensus: Battrick runs seven skills (stamina, batting, bowling, keeping, concentration, consistency, fielding) plus aggression, experience, form and fitness, with per-player attack/normal/defend orders whose effect is stated in one sentence that players treat as canon, "the more aggressive a player, the more likely they are to score runs quickly as a batter, but they will be more likely to get out", and seven pitch types each favouring a bowler class ([Battrick rules](https://www.battrick.org/nl/rules.asp?item=0)).

The open-source landscape is thinner than its star counts suggest. The two most-starred hobby simulators are not reusable: Jamee999/cricket (64 stars) has no licence and Aducj1910's IPLSimulator (38 stars) carries custom "all rights reserved" terms, though its pitch model, random pace/spin/outfield multipliers in a 0.75–1.25 band biased by dusty/green/dead type, with second-innings dew and deterioration, is a legible pattern to re-implement ([GitHub Aducj1910/IPLSimulator](https://github.com/Aducj1910/IPLSimulator)). The MIT-licensed CricVerse Discord bot is the closest open analogue to a commercial engine, with 15 pitch types, ball-age swing and a design statement worth adopting verbatim: "outcome weights are Monte-Carlo tuned so skill decides matches - no freak 250s or 30-all-out collapses between equal sides" ([GitHub CricVerse](https://github.com/TheJaiv/CricVerse)). The only Cricsheet-driven engines with code are the-archer's AGPL random-forest classifier over ODI JSON ([GitHub the-archer/cricket-simulator](https://github.com/the-archer/cricket-simulator)), Ganesh's unlicensed MDP optimiser ([GitHub tvganesh/T20-MDP-optimisation](https://github.com/tvganesh/T20-MDP-optimisation)) and the MIT cricket-mcp TypeScript/DuckDB ingester, which loaded the full archive as 23,080 matches, 11,667,961 deliveries and 15,245 players into a players/matches/innings/deliveries star schema with incremental updates from the `recently_played_N_json.zip` files ([cricket-mcp on GitHub](https://github.com/mavaali/cricket-mcp)). For a TypeScript project that last one is the reference ingest to crib from.

The best-documented practitioner engine sets realistic expectations. Andrew Kuo's 2021 simulator fed each batter's and bowler's historical outcome distribution plus innings state into a 4,508-parameter Keras net emitting eight per-ball classes, Monte-Carloed 500 matches per fixture over 677,291 training balls, reproduced the real distributions of totals, wickets and top scores, and beat Bet365 on log-loss, **0.687 versus 0.695**, while getting the winner right only **55.6% of the time** (a constant 0.5 scores 0.693) with a first-innings simulated standard deviation of 28.6 runs ([Towards Data Science](https://towardsdatascience.com/predicting-t20-cricket-matches-with-a-ball-simulation-model-1e9cae5dea22)). Encoding a player as his empirical outcome vector, rather than a learned embedding or an abstract rating, is both what Kuo did and what maps most naturally onto a card. Broadcast systems work the same way at larger scale: CricViz's WinViz "takes account of the players, the venue and the state of the match to run a series of simulations", driven primarily by career records with venue par scores and, since 2019, a ball-tracking pitch descriptor covering seam, swing, spin and bounce ([Swarajya interview](https://swarajyamag.com/amp/story/sports%2Fswarajya-speaks-to-cricviz-the-team-behind-the-data-intelligence-models-for-cricket-world-cup-2019)). A card game cannot reproduce tracking data, but a per-match pitch vector that scales bowler-type effectiveness mimics it.

The design critique is unanimous and should shape the engine more than any paper. Kotaku calls Cricket Captain "a giant spreadsheet steeped in RNG" where the randomness is "part of the fun" but bad bowling changes and field settings still let you lose ([Kotaku Australia](https://www.kotaku.com.au/?p=908039)); The Cricketer complains that "seemingly fool-proof tactics fail spectacularly" and "there are too few options to play with to change the course of a match" ([The Cricketer](https://www.thecricketer.com/topics/features/cricket_captain's_latest_iteration_remains_an_enjoyable_way_to_lose_track_of_time.html)); and the longest fan wish-list targets the limited-overs engine as "the extension of test matches", with too few singles, chases ending by over 40 and no way to rotate strike ([CC2020 fans thread](https://www.childishthings.com/forum/forum/cricket-captain-2020/69667-things-that-fans-want-to-see-in-cc2020)). The developer responses have been to derive aggression from data, make it follow the chosen tactic rather than the format, and dampen early-innings collapses so "batters settle more quickly" ([CC2022 features](https://www.childishthings.com/forum/forum/cricket-captain-2022/78466-cricket-captain-2022-features-and-steam-page)). Given your playtester's "repetitive, not fun" verdict on the card game, this is the part of the research to take most seriously: a management sim built on a correct per-ball model will still read as a dice-roller unless aggression has a monotone, visible run-rate-versus-dismissal trade-off, bowling changes are tied to pitch type and phase so the counterfactual is readable, and a live score/win predictor shows the player their decision moving the number.

## Build order: one pipeline, three models, five acceptance tests

The data pipeline should be built once, in Node, and treated as a separate workspace from the game. Download `all_json.zip` (147.6 MB, or start with `t20s_json.zip` at 22.9 MB plus `ipl_json.zip` at 5.2 MB) and expect roughly 3 GB if fully extracted, so stream the archive rather than unzipping ([Cricsheet downloads](https://cricsheet.org/downloads/)). Resolve every name through each file's `registry.people` into the 8-character Cricsheet ID, never by display name, because `name` is not unique while `unique_name` and the ID are ([Cricsheet register](https://cricsheet.org/register/)). Aggregate into a SQLite or DuckDB table keyed on (player ID, format, phase, role) with counts of {0, 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, wicket} legal-ball outcomes plus wides, no-balls and byes, where phase is overs 1–6 / 7–15 / 16–20 for T20 and a 1–10 / 11–40 / 41–50 split for ODIs, and store innings-level rows (match, venue, season, total, wickets, balls) alongside for the par-score and venue tables. Map your 332 cards to Cricsheet IDs by hand through `people.csv`'s `unique_name` and `key_cricinfo` columns, committing the mapping as a small JSON next to `cards.json`; at roster scale this is an afternoon, and it sidesteps the Disney scraping problem entirely because you will not need `player_meta` for handedness or bowling type. Add Cricsheet attribution and a link to ODC-By in the in-game credits, the repo README and a `DATA-LICENSE` file, since ODC-By's attribution condition attaches to "works produced from the database" and your published ratings are such a work ([ODC-By summary](https://opendatacommons.org/licenses/by/summary/)). Rebuild from the 2/7/30-day "recently added" zips rather than re-downloading everything, and keep your own cached snapshot because the site is one person's hobby ([Cricsheet downloads](https://cricsheet.org/downloads/)).

Implement the T20 engine first, as a new module beside (not inside) `shared/src/engine/resolve-ball.ts`, because the card game's zone-matching resolution and a probability engine are different objects that should share only the player data. The first model is the Davis–Perera–Swartz structure with Ganesh's simpler shrinkage: for each player and phase compute the empirical seven-outcome vector, shrink it toward the phase mean (or an archetype centroid once you have clustered) with λ = n/(n+50), estimate the global τ(over, wickets) multiplier table directly from Cricsheet cell frequencies, build symmetric bowler vectors, combine as p + q − p̄ with clipping at zero and renormalisation (the paper never says how it handles negatives, so you must), draw extras as a separate Bernoulli at about 5% of deliveries, and implement the chase rule by shifting the batter to a later τ column when his runs-per-resource ratio falls below (target − score + 1)/resources remaining, damped by 0.8 ([t20sim.pdf](https://www.sfu.ca/~tswartz/papers/t20sim.pdf); [arXiv 2604.13861](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.13861)). Layer Bandyopadhyay's bowler-type × phase multipliers on top using your existing `Adjective` field as the type key, which gives you pace-versus-spin texture without per-batter matchup data you do not have ([arXiv 2608.18020](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.18020v2)). The second model is the Stevenson–Brewer eye-in multiplier, two lines in the per-ball loop: scale the batter's dismissal probability by (μ_set + 1)/(μ(x) + 1) with μ(x) = μ_set + (μ_fresh − μ_set)e^{−x/L}, using balls faced as x in limited overs, μ_fresh at roughly 0.25–0.5 of μ_set and L of about 2–6 ([arXiv 1609.04078](https://arxiv.org/abs/1609.04078)). The third is the Delta par-score table g(balls remaining, wickets in hand), a straight empirical mean from your innings table, which serves triple duty as the in-game score predictor, the second-innings pressure signal and the calibration target ([delta-2026.pdf](https://www.cs.columbia.edu/~misra/delta-2026.pdf)). Add an innings-level random effect, a per-match pitch vector in the IPLSimulator ±25% band that multiplies pace, spin and outfield outcomes and decays across the match, before you add anything else, because without it the engine will be under-dispersed exactly as Mishra found ([arXiv 2608.14696](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.14696); [GitHub Aducj1910/IPLSimulator](https://github.com/Aducj1910/IPLSimulator)).

Expose three levers to the player and no more at first: a batting aggression dial that shifts the batter's τ column (so the run-rate/dismissal trade-off is monotone and explainable, per Battrick's rule text), a bowling-change choice whose default is driven by pitch type and phase, and the live par-score/win predictor. That is the minimum set the Cricket Captain community says makes decisions feel causal ([Battrick rules](https://www.battrick.org/nl/rules.asp?item=0); [CC2020 fans thread](https://www.childishthings.com/forum/forum/cricket-captain-2020/69667-things-that-fans-want-to-see-in-cc2020)). Before shipping, run the five acceptance tests the literature implies: Q-Q plots of simulated versus real first-innings totals per team and format; mean wickets lost by over against the real curve; per-ball outcome marginals by phase within total variation 0.02 of Cricsheet; expected remaining runs by (overs, wickets) within about 5 runs of your par-score table and proportional to the D/L curve; and a first-innings standard deviation near the real 28–30 runs so the engine is neither a coin-flip nor a spreadsheet ([t20sim.pdf](https://www.sfu.ca/~tswartz/papers/t20sim.pdf); [Towards Data Science](https://towardsdatascience.com/predicting-t20-cricket-matches-with-a-ball-simulation-model-1e9cae5dea22); [Brooker thesis](https://www.cmi.ac.in/~rlk/Cricket_Analytics/AuxiliaryMaterial/AnEconomicAnalysisofAbilityStrategyandFairnessinODICricket.pdf)). Compute per-ball log-loss too; nobody has published one, so your number becomes the benchmark.

Defer, in this order, ODIs (reuse the T20 machinery with a 50-over τ and Brooker's kinks at balls 48 and 240), then career-mode ageing (quadratic with peak 28–29 for batters and 25–27 for bowlers, slope your choice), then form as a small 0.88–0.96 decay on top of a heavily shrunk career ability, then Tests built from Perera–Gill–Swartz's fourth-innings vectors, geometric wicket rates and the −20% second-team-innings multiplier, and finally fielding, where the per-fielder conversion matrix is elegant but needs commentary text you do not have and is worth only about one run per match per elite fielder ([declare.pdf](https://www.sfu.ca/~tswartz/papers/declare.pdf); [arXiv 1908.11490](https://arxiv.org/abs/1908.11490); [fielding.pdf](https://www.sfu.ca/~tswartz/papers/fielding.pdf)). Use the Cricsheet-derived vectors immediately for one more thing that costs nothing: audit your 332 hand-rated tiers against each player's shrunk expected runs per over and dismissal rate, which is the same comparison the Swartz group ran against ICC ratings and found only r ≈ 0.56–0.61 agreement, so expect, and want, disagreements worth a roster debate ([moneyball.pdf](https://www.sfu.ca/~tswartz/papers/moneyball.pdf)).

## Conclusion

The decision to build is cleaner than it looked a month ago. Cricsheet's October 2026 licence removes the one genuine blocker, the data's legal status, and does so with the most permissive attribution licence available; what remains unresolved is the publicity-rights status of real names, which the data licence was never going to answer and which the entire commercial genre operates without litigating. On the modelling side the research shows there is no secret: twenty years of papers and every practitioner engine reduce to a shrunk per-player outcome vector, a shared situation multiplier, a bowler blend and a handful of multiplicative context terms, all of which are now estimable from a dataset two orders of magnitude larger than the one the original authors had. The intellectual frontier has moved from "how do we model a ball" to "why do state-only models under-disperse", and the answer, innings-level heterogeneity and short momentum, happens to be the same ingredient that makes a game feel like cricket rather than a spreadsheet.

The implication for Swipe Sixer is that the sim engine is the cheap part and the game design is the hard part. The engine described above is a few hundred lines of TypeScript over a few hundred rows of aggregated data, and its correctness can be checked against published tables before a single player sees it. What no paper supplies is the answer to the playtester's complaint: a correct engine will still be "repetitive, not fun" unless the three levers are legible and the predictor shows them working. Build the pipeline and the T20 engine to the acceptance tests, then spend the saved time on the dials.
